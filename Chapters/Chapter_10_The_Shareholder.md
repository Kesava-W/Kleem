# Chapter Ten: The Shareholder

Vikas Limited had lost its third-largest client eighteen months into what Suraj would later come to think of, privately, as the bad years, and he had not thought much of it at the time beyond the ordinary irritation of a contract renewal gone the wrong way. Business did that. Clients left. You absorbed it, adjusted the numbers, moved on.

He thought differently about it when the fourth client left the same year, to the same competitor - a firm called Meridian Systems that none of Suraj's people had heard of before it started underbidding Vikas Ltd by margins that made no financial sense to sustain, not for one contract, and certainly not for four in a row. He had his finance head look into it. She came back with very little: a private company, recently registered, well-funded from somewhere that didn't show up cleanly in the filings, offering terms so aggressive that either Meridian didn't intend to make money on any of these contracts, or didn't need to.

"Somebody's buying market share," his finance head said, which was the obvious explanation and, Suraj suspected even then, not the true one.

By the second year, Vikas Ltd had lost enough business that Suraj found himself doing arithmetic he hadn't done since the company's earliest, leanest days - what could be cut, whose salary could be delayed a month, which loan payment could be pushed without triggering a default clause. He didn't tell Sandhya the full extent of it. He didn't fully admit the full extent of it to himself, some weeks, preferring to believe the next quarter would simply correct itself the way quarters sometimes did.

It did not correct itself.

Sandhya noticed the weight of it before he said a word about it - the light left burning in his study past midnight more nights than not, the particular quiet he carried to the dinner table on the evenings a call hadn't gone the way he'd hoped. She asked, more than once, whether something was wrong at the office. He told her, each time, that it was nothing a good quarter wouldn't fix, which was true often enough in the early months that she had no reason yet to disbelieve it, and false often enough, by the second year, that some part of her had quietly stopped expecting the good quarter to arrive.

---

Niraj came to him near the end of the second year, unannounced, warm in the particular way of an old acquaintance who has heard, through the channels such men always hear things through, that a friend is struggling.

They had known each other for years, the loose, cordial way two men in adjacent industries know each other - dinners at the same functions, a nodding respect neither had ever tested against anything that actually mattered. Niraj sat in Suraj's office, accepted tea he didn't drink, and said, with every appearance of genuine concern, that he'd heard Vikas Ltd was having a difficult run, and that he'd like to help, if Suraj would let him.

"A stake," Niraj said. "Minority. Nothing that touches how you run the company. Just enough capital to get you through whatever this is, from someone who'd rather see an old acquaintance's business survive than watch it get picked apart by people who don't know what you built here."

"Why now," Suraj had asked him, the one question he did think to ask, turning his own untouched cup of tea a slow half-circle on the saucer. "We haven't spoken properly in years."

"Does it matter why, if the help is real?" Niraj had smiled when he said it, easy and unhurried, the smile of a man entirely comfortable with the question being asked and entirely unworried about how he'd answered it. "I've watched you build this from nothing, Suraj. I'd rather help a man I respect than watch vultures do to your company what they did to two others I could name this year alone."

It was, Suraj would think much later, a genuinely well-constructed sentence - true in every particular detail and false in its entire shape, the kind of lie that survived scrutiny precisely because none of its individual parts could be disproven.

Suraj should have asked more questions than the one he'd managed. He knew that, later, with the particular clarity that only arrives after it's no longer useful. At the time, sitting across from a man offering him exactly the amount of money that made the next eighteen months survivable, with terms that seemed, on their face, entirely reasonable, he had felt something close to relief, and relief was not a state of mind that asked many questions.

He signed. The board was glad of it. Word went round, in the loose way word always went round in their circle, that Niraj had done a genuinely generous thing for a struggling peer, and for a while Suraj believed that too, or believed it enough not to look any harder than gratitude required.

---

The looking happened almost by accident, the better part of a year later, during a routine audit ahead of a separate financing round - a junior accountant flagging, without any particular urgency, an inconsistency in Meridian Systems' registration history that had nothing directly to do with Vikas Ltd's own books but had crossed the accountant's desk anyway, filed under the same industry code.

Suraj almost didn't look at it. It was not, strictly, his problem to look at.

He looked at it anyway, some instinct he couldn't have named pulling him toward the filing rather than away from it. The inconsistency itself was small - a director's address on Meridian's original incorporation papers that didn't match the one on their current filings, the kind of clerical gap that meant nothing on its own and everything once you started pulling at it. He asked the accountant, mildly, to pull the historical records rather than the current ones. Then the registrar's archived filings from before the correction. Then, three documents deep into a trail that had clearly been built to discourage exactly this kind of looking, a registered director's address that matched, precisely, a subsidiary of Wolverine Industries.

He sat with the page in front of him for a long time.

It was, once he'd seen it, almost insultingly simple to follow the rest. Meridian Systems, the impossible competitor who had bled Vikas Ltd of its four biggest clients over two ruinous years, funded on terms no honest business could sustain, was Niraj's. Had always been Niraj's. Which meant the man who had walked into Suraj's office at the exact moment the wound was deepest, offering to bind it with his own money, had opened the wound himself, with the same hand, months before he'd ever offered the bandage.

Suraj did not call anyone. He did not confront Niraj, that day or the next, though he turned the idea over more than once, testing the shape of it, the satisfaction it might briefly offer and the considerably longer cost it would certainly extract. A public accusation, unprovable in any court that mattered, against a man who now sat, quite legally, as a shareholder in his own company - it would accomplish nothing beyond confirming, to Niraj and to everyone watching, that Suraj had finally understood the position he was in.

He understood the position he was in. That, more than the discovery itself, was the thing that sat with him longest, in the days that followed - not the anger, though the anger was real, but the plain, cold arithmetic of it. Niraj held a stake in his company now, legally, permanently, bought with money Suraj himself had signed for gratefully. There was no version of undoing that which didn't cost him more than leaving it alone would.

So he left it alone.

There was, underneath the anger, a colder thought he examined only once and then put away deliberately, the way you put away a tool too dangerous to leave lying about: that a man capable of manufacturing an entire crisis to buy his way into a rival's company was a man capable of considerably worse than that, given the right provocation and enough patience to wait for it. Suraj did not know, that year, what shape such a man's worse might eventually take. He only knew, with a certainty he couldn't have explained to anyone who asked, that he had no intention of finding out by provoking him first.

He told no one - not Sandhya, not the board, not even, in the end, the finance head who had first flagged Meridian's strange terms and never learned what became of her question. He simply began, from that week forward, to watch Niraj the way you watch a debt you can't yet pay: aware of it every day, mentioning it to no one, waiting - though for what, exactly, he couldn't have said yet - for the moment it would finally come due.
