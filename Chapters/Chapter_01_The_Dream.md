# Chapter One: The Dream

The evening it happened, nothing about it announced itself.

Sandhya called her twice for dinner before Ananya came, still turning over a case study for her business class that refused to make sense the third time she read it as much as the first. Papa was home early - a small thing, but her mother had already noted it twice, the way she noted anything that broke the pattern of Suraj's evenings, home past nine most nights, Vikas Limited keeping him later than either of them liked to say aloud.

"Leave the case study," Sandhya said, from the doorway. "It will still be wrong after you eat."

Ananya laughed and left it wrong.

Dinner was ordinary in the specific way dinners were ordinary in that apartment - Suraj asking about college with the particular distraction of a man half-listening for his phone, Sandhya correcting the way Ananya held her spoon out of a habit older than Ananya herself, the ceiling fan turning just fast enough to keep the rotis from going cold before anyone finished the first one. Ananya mentioned, between bites, that Sanya's mother had promised them both new bangles for the coming festival, and that Sanya had already decided, with her usual certainty about things that hadn't happened yet, which colour would suit which of them. Sandhya said something about not counting bangles before they were bought. Suraj said nothing, his thumb moving once over the edge of his phone before he set it face-down and gave the table his full attention, as if he'd caught himself at it and didn't care to be caught twice.

Nobody said anything that would be worth remembering later. That was, later, the thing Ananya remembered most about it.

She went to bed at the ordinary time. She fell asleep the ordinary way, mid-thought, the case study's wrong line still unresolved somewhere at the back of her mind.

And then it wasn't ordinary at all.

---

She was somewhere without being anywhere - the way dreams have of putting a person nowhere in particular and everywhere at once. And she was not alone in it.

A woman stood a little apart from her, and light came from the woman rather than falling on her, which Ananya understood without finding strange, the way nothing in a dream is strange until you try afterward to explain it to someone who wasn't there. The woman didn't announce herself. She didn't need to. Ananya knew, the instant she looked at her, the way a person knows they are standing in the sea and not merely near it.

*Marry him,* the woman said - or didn't say; the words arrived the way the light did, from everywhere, needing no mouth to carry them.

And then it began.

A name first, whole and complete, the way a name arrives when you will spend the rest of your life saying it, though you don't know that yet: *Shaktiyuth.*

Then everything at once, and none of it in order.

An infant crying in a house with a garden. A woman in a bed, unwell, her husband's hand not quite steady on the water glass he held for her. A small boy at a low table, his brow furrowed over nothing at all, incurious in a way other children weren't. A woman's face - kind, plain, patient in the specific way patience gets when it has stopped expecting to be thanked for itself - bent down close to his.

*Kleeba,* the woman said to him, a hundred times, a thousand, the number blurring past counting the way numbers do in dreams.

*Kleem,* the boy said back. Always. Never once correctly.

Nothing changed, for a long time. Ananya felt that too - not watched it, felt it, the way you feel the weight of a year passing in a dream in the space of one held breath. A boy slower than the other boys. A boy who didn't play their games, who answered questions with the wrong question back, who sat alone at the edges of things without minding it as much as the adults around him minded it for him.

Then a woman on a friend's rooftop said something cruel-sounding that wasn't meant cruelly - *it isn't easy, a life like that* - and Ananya, still inside it, still nowhere and everywhere, understood that this had already happened to her too, or would.

Then stillness. A dark room. A boy asleep, breathing evenly, a lamp somewhere left low. And the woman who was light rather than lit stood over him and did nothing more than that woman ever seemed to do - she looked. She smiled the way a mother smiles at something finished that isn't finished yet. She touched his hair once, the way you'd touch something you trusted not to need the touching.

Ananya understood this last, without being told: that this had not happened yet. That it would.

Somewhere in it, briefly, a room full of other boys his age, laughing at something Ananya couldn't hear, and the boy at the edge of it, not laughing, not minding, simply waiting out a joke that hadn't been built with room for him in it. Somewhere in it, a woman - not the one made of light, an ordinary woman, tired around the eyes - saying a name that wasn't Kleeba to someone else entirely, and the boy's head turning toward it anyway, as if any shape of that sound belonged to him now.

The images went on a while longer, softer now, half-formed - a badminton racket, a newspaper someone would hold up one day with her own hand though she didn't yet own the hand that would hold it, a tank of water somewhere green and far away that meant nothing to her and everything, she somehow knew, to him. None of it arranged itself into a story she could have repeated word for word. All of it arranged itself, nonetheless, into something she knew completely.

*Marry him,* the woman said again, the words less an instruction now than a fact already true.

And Ananya, somewhere in the dream that was ending, said - or thought, or simply became - *yes.*

---

She woke at half past four, the ceiling fan still turning at the same unhurried speed it always turned at, the room exactly as she'd left it. Nothing about the apartment had changed. She lay still for a while, working out which parts of the last hour had been real in the way a room is real, and found she couldn't - not because the dream was fading, the way dreams usually faded by the time you were properly awake, but because it wasn't fading at all. It sat in her whole and entire, the way the multiplication tables sat in her, learned so early she could no longer remember not knowing them.

She knew his name. She knew the caretaker's name and the word he'd made of it. What she didn't know, and understood clearly that she didn't know, was *when* any of it had happened, or was happening, or would - whether the slow, quiet boy from the clearest parts of the dream was somewhere in the city at this moment, still exactly that boy, or whether he'd already grown into every hazier thing she'd glimpsed after him and simply hadn't been shown to her plainly enough to say so. The disability had arrived whole and certain. Everything past it had arrived like something seen through water - present, moving, unreadable. She understood, without anyone needing to tell her this either, that this not-knowing was itself part of what she'd been given, and that none of it would matter in the end, not to her, not eventually to anyone.

It should have frightened her. She noted, almost with curiosity, that it didn't.

She thought she ought to feel something enormous - falling in love, she'd always assumed from the films Sandhya watched on Sunday afternoons, arrived like weather, sudden and total and visible on the face. She didn't feel that. She noted its absence almost with surprise, the way you'd notice a sound you expected had never actually started. What she felt instead was smaller and far sturdier than love, and came first: the quiet, settled certainty of a fact taking its place in the world. Not *I love him.* Not even *I will.* Only: *this is true, and I will spend a long time finding out what it costs to be right about it.* The rest - whatever the films meant by it, whatever would eventually make his name feel less like something she'd memorised and more like something she'd earned - she understood, without resentment, that she hadn't been given yet. Only the knowing. The feeling, she suspected, was owed to years she hadn't lived through.

She got up once, went to the window, and stood there for no reason she could have named, looking down at a street empty at that hour except for a stray dog working its way along the compound wall. Somewhere out past the last visible streetlight, in a house she'd never seen, a man a year or two older than her - or perhaps, for all she truly knew, still a boy - was entirely, uselessly unaware that a decision involving the rest of his life had just been made several kilometres away, by someone he didn't know existed.

She didn't wake her parents. She didn't call Sanya, though her hand found the phone on the nightstand and held it a moment before setting it back down. Some things, she understood already, without anyone having taught her this either, were not for telling in the middle of the night, half-formed and un-practised, to be gotten wrong in the retelling and never quite right again after that.

Tomorrow. She would tell Sanya tomorrow.

Tonight was only for knowing it herself, sitting with a certainty she had not asked for and would not, she already understood, ever be talked out of - not by Sanya's good sense, not by her parents' bewilderment, not by however many years it took, with nothing more than this to prove her right.

She went back to bed. She did not go back to sleep for a long time, and did not mind that either.

By morning, the case study's wrong line was still wrong. Ananya looked at it, decided it could wait one more day, and went down to breakfast a young woman who knew, with more certainty than she had ever known anything, the name of the man she was going to marry.

Sandhya glanced at her twice over the idli plates, the second time with the particular frown she wore when something about her daughter didn't add up to the sum she expected. "You're quiet," she said. Not a question.

"I slept badly," Ananya said, which was true, and also not the thing that was true, and she was mildly surprised at how easily the smaller truth came to stand in for the larger one. She ate her breakfast. She did not look up the meaning of anything in a dream dictionary, because she already understood, with the same flat certainty that told her the sun would rise from a particular direction, that there was nothing to look up. Some things arrived pre-understood. This was one of them.

She left for college with her bag on one shoulder and the name sitting in her the way a stone sits at the bottom of a clear pond - visible, settled, not going anywhere. She would see Sanya at the gate in twenty minutes. She already knew, with the same certainty as everything else that morning, exactly how that conversation would begin, and had no idea at all how it would end.
