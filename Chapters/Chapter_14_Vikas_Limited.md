# Chapter Fourteen: Vikas Limited

The Meridian account call came in at eleven, which was two hours earlier than Ananya had told the client to expect it, and she took it anyway, because the alternative was letting Rohit take it unprepared and watching him promise something the operations team couldn't actually deliver by month-end.

"You told us Thursday," the man on the other end said, in the clipped, faintly aggrieved tone of someone who had already decided, before the call connected, that he was going to be difficult about whatever answer he got. "It's Thursday. Where is the revised delivery schedule?"

"It's in your inbox as of nine this morning," Ananya said, pulling it up on her own screen as she spoke, keeping her voice level in the specific way she'd learned, over three years of exactly these calls, kept most difficult conversations from becoming more difficult than they needed to be. "I'd also flag, since you have me on the line, that the schedule I sent moves your second shipment two days earlier than we'd originally quoted, not later - I know the subject line probably read like bad news before you'd opened it."

There was a pause on the other end, the sound of someone actually opening an email they'd been prepared to be angry about. "...Oh."

"Take your time," Ananya said, pleasantly, and meant it, because she had learned this too: that most difficult clients weren't difficult people, only frightened ones, and frightened people needed a moment to stop bracing before they could hear anything you actually told them.

He came back apologetic, more or less, in the roundabout way people apologised when they didn't want to say the actual word. Ananya took it graciously and hung up, and sat for a moment afterward with her hand still on the phone, the small, private satisfaction of a fire put out before it had properly started.

"Where did you even learn to do that?" Rohit asked her afterward, not for the first time, with the particular admiration of a colleague two years her senior who still hadn't quite worked out how she managed it. "The bit about the schedule actually being good news. I would have just apologised twice and made it worse."

"You have to read the email before you read the tone of the person calling about it. Most people are angry at what they imagine you did, not what you actually did." She shrugged, already moving on to the next thing on her list. "Half the job is just getting someone to stop bracing long enough to hear you."

Rohit, two desks over, looked faintly relieved. "I thought that one was going to end in a shouting match."

"It nearly did. On his end, not mine." She glanced at the wider floor - quieter than it used to be, she'd noticed over the last year, fewer of the old client names on the whiteboard by the door, a caution in the air that nobody discussed openly but everybody seemed to feel anyway, the way a house feels a draught before anyone thinks to check the window. She didn't know what had caused it. Nobody at her level seemed to. She only knew her own small corner of it: more calls like this one, lately, more clients who needed careful handling rather than routine service, and a general sense, unspoken but persistent, that Vikas Limited was working harder these days to hold on to what it already had than to win anything new.

She didn't dwell on it. It wasn't hers to dwell on, not yet, and there was a report due by four that wasn't going to write itself.

---

She got home later than she meant to, the report having taken longer than the four o'clock deadline strictly allowed for, and found her parents already at the table, an unusual formality to the evening that she registered before she could have said exactly why.

"Sit," Suraj said, once she had, in a tone that told her this dinner had an agenda before he'd said a single further word.

"I've been speaking with Niraj," he said, once the plates had been served and the small talk exhausted itself faster than usual. "Wolverine Industries. You'll know the name."

"I know the name." She didn't know it warmly - a stake in the company, a shareholder she'd met twice at company events and found politely unmemorable both times - but she knew it well enough to feel, immediately, that its arrival at this particular dinner table meant something she wasn't going to enjoy.

"His son, Ankush. I've begun arranging your marriage to him."

The sentence landed in the room the way a dropped plate lands - loud in its own way, though nobody had actually raised their voice, the silence afterward doing more work than any shouting could have. Ananya set her own fork down, carefully, buying herself the half-second the gesture allowed.

"You've *begun*," she repeated. "Without asking me first."

"I'm asking you now."

"That's not the same thing, Papa, and you know it." She kept her voice level, the same trained evenness she'd used on the Meridian call that morning, though this cost her considerably more to hold onto. "I'm not marrying Ankush."

Suraj's expression didn't change, exactly, but something in it sharpened - not anger, she thought, watching him carefully. Something closer to a man recalculating a plan mid-sentence. "You don't know him well enough to refuse him this quickly."

"You've met him twice, Papa. At a function. You told me yourself you couldn't remember a single thing he'd said either time."

"That's not the point of an arranged match, and you know it isn't."

"Then what is the point of it? Because I don't need it, whatever it is. I already have someone."

Suraj set down his own fork now, giving her his full attention in the particular way he rarely did, the way she associated more with his office than his dinner table. "Someone your mother and I have never once heard mentioned in this house."

"I don't need to know him. I already know who I'm marrying." She said it plainly, the way she'd said it to Sanya in a library six years ago, the way she'd carried it every day since without once needing anyone else's belief to hold it steady. "His name is Shaktiyuth."

Something crossed her father's face at the name - there and gone so quickly she might have imagined it, if she hadn't been watching him closely enough to catch it at all. Not confusion. Recognition, she thought, though he said nothing to confirm or explain it, and the moment passed too fast for her to be certain she'd read it correctly.

"A Goddess came to me in a dream, six years ago, and told me to marry him," Ananya said, into the silence that followed, and told them the rest of it plainly - the caretaker, the mispronunciation, the disability, the newspaper six weeks past that had finally, after six years, put a face and a business and a whole thriving life to a name she'd carried since she was nineteen.

Sandhya was the first to find her voice, and what came out of it was closer to alarm than argument. "Ananya. You cannot build a marriage on a dream."

"I'm not building it on a dream, Ma. The dream was six years ago. Everything since has only ever proven it right."

"It's still a dream. You're speaking as though a Goddess visiting you in your sleep is an ordinary thing to plan a life around -"

"Then be careful how you speak about Her," Ananya said, and the room went very still around the words, "because if She's real - and I know She's real, the way I know my own name - then you don't want to be the one in this family who dismissed Her to my face."

Sandhya's mouth opened and closed once, no sound emerging either time, her eyes flicking to Suraj as though he might have some instruction for how to receive a warning like that from her own daughter. He offered none. He sat very still himself, watching Ananya with an expression she couldn't fully read - not angry, not frightened exactly, something more careful than either, the look of a man quietly re-measuring a decision he'd thought was already settled.

"Go to your room," he said, finally, not unkindly, but with a finality that closed the conversation rather than continuing it. "We'll speak more of this tomorrow."

Ananya went, because there was nothing left to say that the evening would actually hear, and because some part of her - the same part that had known his name in a dream six years before she'd had any proof of it - understood, climbing the stairs, that her father's stillness back there had not been the stillness of a man certain he was right.

She sat on the edge of her bed for a long time afterward, listening to the low, indistinct murmur of her parents' voices still going on downstairs, too quiet through the floor to make out a single word of it. She thought about going back down and asking, plainly, what exactly had crossed her father's face when she'd said the name - whether he knew something about Shaktiyuth she didn't, or only recognised the name from some business circular she'd never see. She decided against it. Whatever it was, he hadn't offered it freely, and she had learned, watching him for twenty-five years, that Suraj only ever told you a thing once he'd finished deciding, entirely on his own terms, that you needed to know it.

She would find out eventually. She always did, with him. She just hadn't yet worked out whether eventually would arrive before the wedding he'd already begun arranging, or after.
