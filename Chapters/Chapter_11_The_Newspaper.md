# Chapter Eleven: The Newspaper

Six years is a long time to stop thinking about something without ever quite putting it down.

Sanya had her own life by then - a job in HR at a firm two floors up from where she'd once imagined, at twenty, she might end up; a flat she shared with a colleague who left for the gym before either of them was properly awake; a Sunday morning routine built around the newspaper, a second cup of chai, and whatever thirty unhurried minutes the week could spare her before it started making its usual demands. She still saw Ananya most weeks - dinner, sometimes, or a phone call that ran long the way their calls always had, since school - and the name Shaktiyuth had not come up between them in longer than either of them could have precisely dated. Sanya assumed, when she thought about it at all, that whatever Ananya still carried, she carried it quietly now, the way you carry most things after enough years have passed.

She was on her second cup that Sunday, the business section folded open more out of habit than interest, when a face on page four stopped her hand halfway to the cup.

The photograph was unremarkable as photographs went - a man in his mid-twenties, dark-eyed, unsmiling in the particular way people are unsmiling when a photographer has asked them to look serious rather than happy, standing in front of what the caption identified as a dairy operation two hours outside the city. The headline ran above it: *Four Friends, One Field: The Quiet Rise of Kleem Dairy & Agri and Revati Systems.* Sanya read the name under the photograph twice before it landed properly.

**Shaktiyuth**, 27, co-founder.

She read the piece the way you read something you don't yet believe applies to you - fast, then slower, then a third time, word by word, once the shape of it had started to matter. A business daily's profile of four college friends who'd built four small companies out of one borrowed field. A paragraph, four-fifths of the way down, that the journalist had clearly included for colour rather than substance, the kind of detail that made a founder's story a story rather than a balance sheet: *Shaktiyuth, whose childhood was marked by a severe developmental disability that doctors once believed might be lifelong, credits his family's unwavering support and, in his words, "a very patient woman who never once gave up on a slow morning," for whatever he's since managed to build.*

Sanya set the cup down without drinking from it.

She did not, in that first minute, think about business daily circulation figures or coincidence or how many men in the country might share both a disability and this exact, unusual name. She thought, with a clarity that arrived faster than she could examine it, of a library bench under a gulmohar tree, six years gone, and a girl saying *his name is Shaktiyuth* in a voice that had not sounded, even then, like a girl repeating something from a dream she wasn't sure she believed herself.

She sat with it a moment longer than she meant to, turning the paper slightly under the window light as though a different angle might change what the words said. It didn't. She thought, briefly and a little uselessly, of ringing Ananya instead - it would have been the easier thing, certainly the faster one - and dismissed the idea almost as soon as it arrived. Some news, she'd learned over fifteen years of friendship, wasn't built for a phone call. This felt, sitting there with the paper still damp at the fold from her own thumb, like news that needed a face in front of it.

She photographed the page with her phone, twice, to be certain the first one hadn't come out blurred, and left the house with her chai still full and going cold on the table behind her.

---

Ananya was at her desk when Sanya arrived at Vikas Limited's reception unannounced, badge-less, waved through only because the security guard at the gate had known her face for six years and stopped asking questions about it around year three.

She must have looked as urgent as she felt, because Ananya was on her feet before Sanya had finished crossing the office floor, already asking what was wrong, already scanning her for the kind of bad news a Sunday-into-Monday visit like this usually meant.

Sanya didn't answer. She put the phone down on the desk between them instead, the photograph of page four still open on the screen, and watched Ananya's eyes go to it, and waited.

She had expected many things from this moment, turning it over the whole drive there without meaning to. Tears, perhaps. Disbelief. Some vindicated, furious *I told you so* aimed squarely at six years of Sanya's own careful skepticism. What she had not expected, and what she got instead, was stillness - Ananya reading the paragraph once, then again, her face giving away almost nothing beyond a very slight, very private softening around the eyes, the look of someone confirming an address she'd already memorised rather than discovering one for the first time.

"That's him," Ananya said. Not a question.

"You don't sound surprised."

"I'm not." She set the phone down, gently, the way you set down something you don't want to risk dropping even though it isn't actually fragile. "I've known his name for six years, Sanya. I've known his caretaker's name. I've known he was disabled and I've known - not clearly, not the way I know this - that it wouldn't stay that way." She looked back up, and there was something in her face now that Sanya had genuinely never seen on it before, not in fifteen years of friendship: not triumph, not relief exactly, something quieter and heavier than either. "I wasn't waiting to be right. I want you to understand that, because I know how it must have looked from outside, all this time. I wasn't sitting around hoping for proof. I already had everything I needed."

"Then what is this?" Sanya gestured at the phone, unable to keep a small, disbelieving laugh out of the question. "What do you call this, if not proof?"

"A photograph." Ananya almost smiled, saying it - the first real crack in the stillness since Sanya had arrived. "I'm glad of it. I'm not going to pretend I'm not. There's a difference between glad and surprised, and I need you, of all people, to understand which one this actually is."

Sanya sat down slowly in the chair across the desk, some of the urgency going out of her all at once, replaced by something closer to the careful attention she'd once brought to a library bench years ago. "Six years," she said, half to herself. "You really never once doubted it."

"I doubted plenty of things. Whether I was making a fool of myself. Whether I'd ever actually meet him, or just spend a whole life quietly certain about a stranger." Ananya looked back down at the small photograph, at the serious, unsmiling face of a man she had never met and had, in every way that counted to her, already known for six years. "I never doubted this part. That was never the part in question."

"I don't know yet," she said, finally, honestly. "I think I need a little time to work out what it is."

---

On her way home that evening, Ananya stopped at the stall near the bus stand and bought her own copy of the paper, a day late and slightly creased from the bundle, and stood there on the pavement for a moment holding it up to the streetlight to read the paragraph again in her own hand rather than on a borrowed screen - an ordinary, unremarkable thing to do, and the last small piece, she thought, folding it away into her bag, of a picture she'd been shown six years ago in fragments too hazy to have named at the time.

She did not cry that morning, though Sanya, watching her carefully across the desk, half expected it to arrive later, in private, the way delayed things sometimes did. It did not, as far as Sanya ever learned. What she noticed instead, over the weeks that followed, was something quieter and harder to name - a kind of settling, in Ananya, the way a house settles once a long-expected weight finally arrives to rest on its foundation. She went to work. She came home. She did not, as far as Sanya could tell, do anything at all differently, except that she now carried herself like a woman who had stopped waiting for a fact and started living, instead, inside the certainty of one.

Sanya kept the photograph on her phone for longer than she probably needed to, opening it every so often in an idle moment, not out of doubt anymore but something closer to disbelief at her own six years of doubt - at how completely she'd underestimated a friend she'd known since the third standard, and how little that friend had ever needed her to be convinced in order to keep believing something entirely on her own.
