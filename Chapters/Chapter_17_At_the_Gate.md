# Chapter Seventeen: At the Gate

Three days after the dinner where her father had said Ankush's name over the daal as though it were already settled, nothing else had moved. Suraj hadn't raised the subject again. Sandhya had grown carefully, deliberately cheerful in the mornings, the particular cheerfulness of a woman who had decided not to ask a question she didn't want answered. Ananya had gone to work each of those three days exactly as she always did, because there was nothing else to do with an arrangement nobody had actually cancelled and nobody had actually confirmed, and a report still didn't write itself just because your life had quietly become someone else's negotiation.

Thursday was a slow morning by Vikas Limited's usual standards - no client fires, no Rohit hovering by her desk with a half-panicked question - and she'd used the lull to clear her inbox down to nothing, which was rare enough that she noticed it herself. She got up to refill her water bottle a little after eleven, and it was on the way back from the cooler, glancing out of the third-floor window the way you glance out of any window you pass a hundred times a day without expecting it to hold anything, that she saw him.

A man, standing at the gate. Nothing about the shape of him should have meant anything to her. He wasn't doing anything - not calling out, not approaching the security cabin, just standing where the visitors' path met the road, hands loose at his sides, looking up at the building the way a person looks at an address they've only ever known as a name. She kept walking for two more steps before whatever had actually registered caught up with the rest of her, and by then her feet had already stopped on their own, water bottle forgotten in her hand, and something in her chest had gone very still and very loud at the same time.

She didn't know his face. That was the strange part, the part she'd turn over for years afterward - the dream had never given her his face, only pieces, a photograph shown a fraction of a second at a time from too many angles to assemble, and every image she did have clearly was a boy, small and slow and nine years old, not whatever this was standing at her office gate in an ordinary shirt with his sleeves pushed up. She had no right to know him. And she knew him anyway, the way you know your own name before you've thought to ask what it is, a recognition that arrived a full breath ahead of any evidence for it and left her standing in the middle of the third floor with her heart going as though she'd already been running.

She put the bottle down on the nearest desk - Priyanka's, she registered dimly, and would apologise for it later - and went.

She didn't take the lift. She took the stairs, two and sometimes three at a time, one hand skimming the rail without gripping it, and felt the specific, unglamorous reality of it in her whole body - the catch in her chest by the second flight, the way her sandals weren't built for this and she didn't care, a stitch starting low on her right side that she ran straight through rather than around. She passed Rohit on the second-floor landing, who said something to her she never afterwards remembered a single word of, and didn't stop to explain herself to security either, just called out something that might have been a name and might have been nothing at all as she went through the lobby doors and out into the sun.

The heat hit her first - eleven o'clock heat, the kind that sat on your shoulders like a hand - and then the gravel of the visitors' path under her sandals, uneven enough that she nearly turned an ankle and kept going anyway, and the whole width of the front lawn between her and the gate, further than she'd ever registered it being on any ordinary day she'd walked it at half this speed. He'd seen her by then. She watched him start moving too, not running the way she was, something more contained than that, and some distant, still-functioning part of her mind noted even in that moment that this was exactly like him already - measured, even now, even here - before the rest of her stopped noting anything at all and simply closed the last of the distance.

They reached each other somewhere on the lawn, not quite at the gate, not quite at the building, a patch of grass that would mean nothing to anyone else ever again. She was smiling before she'd decided to. She could feel that she was sweating - the run, the heat, both - and that her eyes had gone hot and wet in a way she made no attempt to blink back, and none of it embarrassed her even slightly. For a moment neither of them said anything at all. They simply stood there, a little out of breath, close enough now that she could finally, actually see him - the whole assembled shape of a face the dream had only ever handed her in pieces, resolving all at once into a single ordinary, extraordinary man looking back at her as though he'd been doing the same arithmetic on her face that she'd just finished doing on his.

"Anu," he said.

It was the first time anyone had ever called her that. It landed exactly the way the name in her own head, six years unspoken to another living soul, had always sounded when she said it to herself in the dark - which should have been impossible, and wasn't, and she didn't waste a single second wondering how.

"Shakti," she said back, and heard her own voice do something it hadn't done in front of anyone since she was a child - crack, just slightly, on a single syllable.

Then he closed the last half-step between them and she was in his arms, both of hers coming up around him before she'd consciously decided that either, her face turned into his shoulder, his shirt slightly damp with the walk he'd made to get here from wherever he'd come, both of them laughing now in the particular helpless way people laugh when crying would be just as accurate and slightly more work. She felt him breathe out once, long, against the top of her head, the sound of a held thing finally set down.

"You're early," she said into his shoulder, muffled, not caring that it made no sense.

"I've been early for six years," he said, and she felt more than heard the small, dry amusement in it, close enough to her ear that it landed like something private. "I was only waiting to know where to stand."

She pulled back far enough to look at him properly - really look, the way she imagined she'd been looking at him in her sleep for six years without ever once getting to check her own memory against the real thing. He was taller than she'd have guessed from nothing at all, which was a foolish thing to notice and she noticed it anyway; there was a stillness to him that had nothing to do with shyness, a way of holding his own attention that made her feel, absurdly, like the most closely observed thing on the entire street. His eyes were kind in a manner she hadn't expected to be able to confirm from a dream and could now, apparently, confirm perfectly well.

"I don't even know your surname," she said, half-laughing, wiping under one eye with the back of her wrist.

"Neither does half the city, as it happens," he said. "I've never felt the lack of it. Ask me something that actually matters."

"All right." She considered him, still holding both his forearms, unwilling yet to let either hand go. "Did you know. Before the dream ever reached me. Did you know any of this was coming."

"No," he said, without hesitation, which she found she believed instantly and completely. "I found out three days ago, standing under a tree in my own garden, same as everyone else in this story appears to be finding things out roughly three days at a time." A small, private smile crossed his face at his own joke, gone almost as soon as it arrived. "I came as soon as I understood where to come."

"You could have called first."

"I don't have your number," he said, entirely reasonably, and she laughed again, properly this time, the sound coming out of her loose and unguarded in a way it hadn't in longer than she could easily place.

"There's something else," he said, after a moment, the playfulness in him settling into something steadier. "I know about Ankush. And the marriage your father's arranging."

Her smile faltered, just slightly. "How."

"The same way I found this gate. I asked." He said it plainly, without any theatre in it. "A man like Niraj isn't difficult to learn things about, if you know which questions to put to which driver rather than which minister. I've known for two days. I would have come regardless of what I found, but I didn't want you thinking I'd walked in here without understanding what I was walking into."

"You could have led with that."

"I wanted to see your face first," he said, "before either of us had to think about his."

They stood there a while longer, not moving toward the building, not moving toward the gate, simply occupying the small patch of lawn between the two as though it had been built for exactly this and nothing else - trading questions that mattered less for their answers than for the plain fact of being allowed to ask them at all, her hand finding its way at some point into his without either of them appearing to have decided it should. Somewhere behind her, she was faintly aware, the ordinary machinery of Vikas Limited was continuing to run without her - calls she wasn't taking, a report she wasn't writing - and she found, standing there, that she could not have named a single thing about that morning's inbox if her life had depended on it.

It was Shaktiyuth's gaze that shifted first, some small alteration in his stillness that she felt rather than saw, his attention moving fractionally past her shoulder toward the road. She turned to follow it and saw nothing there worth the look - a car pulling in through the far gate, unremarkable, one of a dozen that used that entrance on any given morning. She turned back to him, and whatever he'd noticed, he didn't say.

Ankush had come to Vikas Limited that morning for no better reason than the one his father had given him - a shareholder's son, checking in on an investment that was, as far as Wolverine Industries was concerned, now partly his family's business to watch over, and partly, though he wouldn't have said this aloud even to himself, an excuse to be seen walking these halls as though he already belonged in them. He was halfway across the car park, adjusting his cuffs in the way he did before any room he expected to be looked at in, when he saw them on the lawn - a man he didn't know, and beside him, laughing, her whole face turned up and open in a way he had personally never once managed to put there in two brief, forgettable meetings, Ananya.

He stopped walking. He watched them a moment longer than he needed to, long enough to see the man say something and Ananya laugh again, easy, unguarded, her hand still in his - and something crossed Ankush's own face in that moment that would have surprised anyone who expected only anger from him: not jealousy, not yet, or not only that, something closer to recognition, the specific ache of watching a stranger be effortlessly, unselfconsciously certain of himself in a way Ankush had spent his whole life practising in mirrors and never once managed to make look easy.

Ankush's eyes, when Suraj's own security guard glanced over and happened to catch sight of his face a moment later, had gone entirely red.
