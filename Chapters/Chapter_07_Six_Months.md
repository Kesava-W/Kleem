# Chapter Seven: Six Months

It began with the shoelaces.

Kleeba noticed it the way she noticed most things about him, sideways, without meaning to - crouching down out of long habit one morning to help, and finding both laces already tied, evenly, without the lopsided bow he usually managed on his better days. She straightened up and looked at him properly. He looked back at her the way he always did, unbothered, already reaching for his schoolbag, and said nothing about it because there was nothing, as far as he seemed to know, to say.

She didn't mention it to Revati that evening. It seemed too small a thing to carry across a doorway, and she had learned, over nine years in that house, not to make too much of a single good morning. Boys had good mornings. It didn't always mean anything.

---

A fortnight later, his teacher stopped Kleeba at the school gate - a woman Kleeba had spoken to perhaps four times in as many years, always about the same small list of concerns - and said, with a carefulness that suggested she didn't quite trust her own observation, that Shaktiyuth had answered a question in class that morning. Not repeated an answer. Answered one, correctly, unprompted, in a way that had made the rest of the room go briefly quiet.

"Which question," Kleeba asked, because it seemed important to know.

The teacher couldn't quite remember the exact wording. Only that it had surprised her enough to mention it at all, which was itself the more telling detail.

Kleeba walked him home that afternoon turning the moment over without quite knowing what to do with it. He walked beside her the way he always walked, quiet, unhurried, and when she asked him, carefully, whether school had gone well that day, he said, "Yes," in the same flat, unremarkable tone he used for everything, and offered nothing further, and she let it go, because pressing him had never once produced more than he was ready to give.

---

By the second month it was no longer a single morning, or a single answer in a single classroom. It was a pattern, and patterns were the one thing nine years of this work had trained Kleeba to see before anyone else in the house did.

He finished his homework faster. He held his pencil differently, without the small, familiar struggle that had marked every letter he'd ever written - not perfectly, not yet, but easily, the way you hold a thing you've simply stopped fighting. When his routine changed unexpectedly - a delayed bus, a rearranged room at school - he did not disappear into himself the way he once had. He stood a moment, visibly working something out, and then adjusted, the way other children adjusted, the way she had watched other mothers' sons adjust for years while quietly hoping, without ever quite admitting she was hoping, that hers might learn to do the same one day.

She said nothing to Saketh and Revati yet. Some part of her - she couldn't have named it exactly, wasn't sure she wanted to - was waiting to see whether it would hold.

---

It held.

By the fourth month, Shaktiyuth had joined the badminton club that met behind the school on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons, on his own initiative, without anyone suggesting it to him. Kleeba went once to watch, standing at the fence with the other mothers she'd never quite belonged among, and saw him return a shot with a precision that made the boy across the net stop and stare for a full second before serving again. She thought, watching him move, of a summer years back - a borrowed racket, a shuttle sent clean over a compound wall, a swing he hadn't touched again for two years afterward - and understood, without being able to explain how she understood it, that this was not a new skill arriving from nowhere. It was an old one, finally being allowed to be used.

He made a friend that month too - a boy from the badminton club, ordinary in every way, who began appearing at the gate some afternoons to walk home partway with him. Kleeba watched them from the veranda once, the two of them laughing at something she couldn't hear, and felt something in her chest that she recognised, eventually, as a particular and complicated kind of happiness - the kind that comes with its own small loss folded inside it, because a boy who had a friend of his own no longer needed, quite so completely, a woman who had been the whole of his world for as long as either of them could remember.

She asked him once, carefully, what the joke had been. He tried to explain it to her - something about a teacher's expression, a mistimed answer, a sequence of small things that had apparently built, in the retelling, to something genuinely funny - and lost the thread of it halfway through his own explanation, laughing at the memory of it instead of finishing the story, in a way that struck Kleeba as more remarkable than the joke itself could possibly have been. A year before, he would not have understood why the other boys were laughing at all, would have stood at the edge of it the way he stood at the edge of every game, patient and uncomprehending. Now he was inside the joke, catching its rhythm before it landed, the way anyone catches a rhythm they've simply had enough practice hearing.

---

By the sixth month, there was no pretending any of it was ordinary any longer.

His marks came home near the top of his class. His teachers, the same ones who had spoken so carefully around his difficulties for years, now spoke of him with an entirely different carefulness - the kind reserved for a child they were beginning to suspect they'd underestimated. He read for pleasure now, in the evenings, books well past what anyone would have handed an eleven-year-old a year before. He still had his quiet moments - a stillness that came over him sometimes, unprompted, that Kleeba recognised from the old days and had learned not to interrupt - but they came rarer now, and passed faster, and no longer seemed to frighten him the way they once had.

Saketh and Revati, when they finally sat with it properly one evening over dinner, did not ask Kleeba to explain what had changed, because there was nothing she could have told them that either of them would have believed, and because - this was the part that surprised her most - neither of them seemed to want an explanation half so much as they simply wanted to be glad. Revati cried a little, at the table, unashamed of it, and Saketh reached over and held her hand and said, to both of them, "Whatever this is. Thank god for it," and that, as far as either parent was concerned, was the whole of the matter settled.

It was Revati who raised the other thing, a week later, gently, in the kitchen while Kleeba was finishing the evening dishes.

"He doesn't need looking after the way he did," Revati said. Not unkindly. Simply true. "Not all day, every day. Not anymore."

Kleeba kept drying the plate in her hands a moment longer than it needed. "No," she agreed. "He doesn't."

"We'd like you to stay. Not as - " Revati searched for the word, careful with it. "Not the same work. We're expanding the office next quarter. We'd like you there, properly, if you'd have it. You've more than earned a place in this family beyond that boy's bedroom door."

Kleeba said yes, because it was a good offer, kindly meant, and because she understood - better, perhaps, than either of the boy's own parents did - that some chapters simply ended when they ended, whether or not you were entirely ready for them to.

"You'll still see him," Revati added, gently, reading something in Kleeba's face that Kleeba hadn't meant to let show. "Every day, most likely. He's not going anywhere. Neither are you."

"I know," Kleeba said, and meant it, and still felt the small, private ache of it anyway - not sorrow, exactly. Closer to the particular soreness a muscle feels once a long labour is finally, genuinely finished.

That night, before she left, she looked in on him once more the way she had a thousand nights before, and found him already asleep, breathing evenly, the room around him arranged the particular way it always was - puzzles stacked by size, frames hung exactly straight, the door left open the width he liked. He looked, sleeping there, almost exactly like the boy she'd first met nine years ago.

Almost.

"Kleem," she said, very quietly, to a room that couldn't hear her, and let herself smile at how foolish it was to feel like crying over a boy who was, by every possible measure, finally, wonderfully fine.
