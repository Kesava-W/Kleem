# Chapter Twenty-Four: What They Learned

Two days after the water tank, Shaktiyuth came home with a woman neither of his parents had ever met, and Revati knew, watching her son cross the garden with someone's hand held tightly in his, before a single word had been said, that whatever this visit was going to be, it was not going to be an ordinary one.

"Ananya," Revati said, before her son had managed a single word of introduction, and held both the girl's hands in her own for a long moment on the veranda steps, the particular greeting she gave anyone she'd already, privately, decided to love. "I feel I ought to apologise for not knowing your name sooner. Apparently you've known mine for six years."

"I've known everyone's names for six years," Ananya said, with a small, tired laugh that had more relief in it than humour. "It's very strange, meeting you all properly, after imagining it for so long."

Saketh brought out chairs onto the veranda without being asked, the way he did whenever a conversation announced itself as needing somewhere proper to happen, and the four of them sat there through the slow gold end of the afternoon while Shaktiyuth told them everything - the dream Ananya had carried for six years before she'd ever seen his face, the marriage arranged against her, the night before the wedding, the tank. He told it plainly, without dressing any of it up or down, the same register he'd have used to walk a client through a difficult quarter, and Revati found herself watching her son's face more than listening to his words, still relearning, even now, years after the boy who couldn't stack blocks had become this, exactly how much of him she still recognised and exactly how much she didn't.

He told them about Niraj last.

Saketh went very still when he heard the name - stiller, Revati thought, than the story so far had earned on its own, the two men in the tale being, until that moment, simply Ananya's father and a stranger's. "Niraj," he repeated, testing the shape of it as though it might, on a second saying, turn out to belong to someone else entirely. "Wolverine Industries."

"You knew him," Shaktiyuth said. It wasn't a question.

"Years ago. Before you were born, and for a little while after." Saketh's hands, folded in his lap, had gone very quiet. "We built our companies up together, in a manner of speaking - two young men who thought they understood exactly how far ahead of everyone else they were going to get. We were close, for a time. Closer than either of us was, I think, with most other people we knew in those years."

"He poisoned Maa," Shaktiyuth said. "The sweet, when she was pregnant with me. He told me himself, two nights ago, before he died of it."

The garden went very quiet. Somewhere past the wall a bird Revati couldn't have named called twice and stopped.

Revati had spent twenty-seven years, on and off, wondering about that illness - not obsessively, not in any way that had shaped her days, but as a small, unresolved arithmetic problem she'd occasionally, idly, failed to solve at two in the morning: why that year, why that particular fever, why it had taken hold the way it had and never fully let go of the child growing inside her while it did. She had made her peace with not knowing a long time ago, the way you make your peace with any question that seemed to have no further evidence coming. She found, hearing the answer finally arrive after all this time, that the peace held better than she'd have guessed it would - not because the answer didn't matter, but because grief, it turned out, was not the same shape twice. This one arrived quiet rather than sharp, the particular ache of a wound so old it had long since stopped being new news to the body carrying it, however new it was to the mind only just now being told.

"Why," Saketh said, finally, and it wasn't really a question either, more a word he needed to say aloud before he could move past it. "We were friends. Whatever else either of us became since, we were friends first."

"Jealousy," Shaktiyuth said. "Your company was outpacing his. That's the whole of it, as far as he told it. There isn't a better reason underneath it. I looked, once, a long time ago, for one, and never found it."

Saketh nodded slowly, and said nothing further for a while, turning something over behind a face that had gone carefully, deliberately composed. Revati, watching him, understood without being told what he was doing - not disbelieving it, not even truly surprised by it, but reassembling twenty-seven years of a friendship's quiet, unexplained cooling into a shape that finally made sense, each small distance she'd half-noticed over the years and never once asked him about now sliding into a place that had clearly been waiting for it all along.

"I always wondered why he stopped coming around," Saketh said, eventually, to no one in particular. "Not all at once. Slowly, over a year or two, the way people drift when neither one wants to be the first to say the friendship's over. I remember he stopped coming to the house the same year you were born - sent gifts instead, always generous, always somehow arriving through a driver rather than his own hand. I told myself it was simply business, two men getting busier, going different directions. I never once let myself wonder whether it was something worse than that." He looked toward his son. "I'm not sure whether that makes me a poor friend, or a poor judge of one."

Nobody answered him. It was, Revati thought, watching her husband sit with a question he'd clearly meant to be rhetorical and clearly wasn't, exactly the kind of silence he'd have filled himself for anyone else at this table, and precisely the one he seemed least equipped to fill for his own sake. Ananya, she noticed, opened her mouth once as though to offer him something, and then didn't, watching him with an expression that looked less like withholding comfort than like understanding, correctly, that comfort wasn't actually what the question had been asking for.

Revati reached over, without quite deciding to, and took her husband's hand, and he let her, and for a while none of them said anything at all, the garden filling in around the silence with its ordinary evening sounds - the same guava tree, she thought, distantly, that Shaktiyuth had once told her, in an unguarded moment years ago, he sometimes stood under at night for reasons he'd never fully explained to her.

"What happens now?" Revati asked, finally, because someone needed to be the one to turn the conversation back toward the living. "For the two of you."

Shaktiyuth glanced at Ananya, and something passed between them that needed no further translation for anyone watching it. "We'll marry properly," he said. "Once enough time has passed that it doesn't look like what it actually is. Her father will need that much, at least, to manage everything else he's left holding."

"And after that?"

"After that," Ananya said, and there was something in her voice - tired, and steady, and entirely unafraid - that Revati recognised immediately as the exact tone of a woman who had already spent six years waiting for a life she'd never once doubted was coming. "After that, I think we simply begin it."
