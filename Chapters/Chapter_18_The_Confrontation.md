# Chapter Eighteen: The Confrontation

She was still faintly smiling when she got home that evening, the kind of smile that had outlasted the day itself and settled somewhere under her skin where she couldn't quite call it back even when she tried. She'd spent the drive replaying small, useless details - the damp shoulder of his shirt, the dry way he'd said *I've been early for six years* - the way you replay something not because you're trying to remember it but because some part of you is still standing there, refusing to be anywhere else yet. She'd meant to tell her parents everything the moment she walked in, all of it, unhurried and complete, the way she'd never quite managed to at that first dinner. She didn't get the chance.

It was that same smile, still on her face, that told her something was wrong before anyone had said a single word to her - the wrongness announcing itself first as a kind of silence, the flat, listening quiet of a house that had been rearranging itself around bad news since before she'd turned the key. Sandhya was standing in the hallway rather than sitting anywhere sensible, wringing the end of her dupatta between two fingers in the way she did only when a thing had already gone further than she knew how to stop. Suraj was in the front room, on his feet instead of in his chair, facing the door.

"They're on their way up," he said, before she'd even set down her bag.

"Who -"

The bell answered for him. Suraj opened the door himself, which he almost never did with staff in the house, and Niraj came in first - unhurried, dressed as though for a business meeting rather than whatever this actually was, a small, courteous nod for Suraj and a longer, more deliberate look for Ananya that lasted exactly one beat too long to be simple politeness. Ankush came in behind him already talking, his voice pitched loud enough that it filled the hallway before he'd properly crossed the threshold.

"You want to explain to me," he said, straight at Ananya, not troubling himself with a greeting first, "who that man was. At your office. This morning."

"Ankush." Suraj's voice, placating, already moving to stand half between them. "Let's sit first -"

"I don't want to sit." Ankush's face had gone a dull, blotched red that had nothing elegant about it, the colour of a man more embarrassed than truly wounded, though he'd have argued the two apart if anyone had suggested it to his face. "I came here to hear it from her. Not from you."

"His name is Shaktiyuth," Ananya said, and kept her voice level in the exact way she'd used on the Meridian call four days before, though this cost her a great deal more to hold onto than any client ever had. "I told my parents about him before any of this marriage business began. I'm sorry you're only hearing it now, but you're not hearing anything I haven't already said in this house."

"You were *laughing* with him. In front of the entire staff. In front of anyone who cared to look." Ankush's hands had come up, not quite a gesture at her, more a gesture at the whole unbearable memory of that morning. "Do you have any idea how that looks? For me?"

"I wasn't thinking about how it looked," Ananya said. "I was thinking about him."

Something flickered across Ankush's face at that - not anger this time, something quicker and less guarded, gone almost before she'd registered it. It occurred to her, watching him, that most of what was happening in this room had very little to do with her at all. Whatever he'd walked in braced to feel, it hadn't looked like this from where he'd been standing that morning - a man laughing easily with a woman who'd never once laughed that way at him, unhurried and entirely at ease in his own skin in a manner Ankush had never once, in her limited experience of him, managed to look for longer than the length of a toast at someone else's function. Ananya suspected, watching the flicker pass, that Ankush had understood exactly what he was envying before he'd had time to decide he was furious about it instead.

It was, she registered even as she said it, probably the least diplomatic sentence available to her in that moment, and she found she didn't especially regret it. Sandhya made a small, strangled sound from the doorway that wasn't quite a word. Suraj didn't move, didn't speak, watching his daughter with an expression she couldn't fully read - not disapproval, not quite; something closer to a man recalculating, again, a plan he'd already begun to suspect wasn't going to survive contact with reality.

It was Niraj, still standing just inside the door with his hands loosely folded, who spoke next, and his voice, when it came, was so mild by comparison to his son's that it took the room a moment to properly register it. "There's no need for anyone to raise their voice in your own home, Suraj-ji." He said it lightly, almost kindly, the way a man might comment on the weather while standing over something he intended to break regardless of what the weather actually did. "These things happen. A girl her age, a moment's foolishness. It needn't become more than that."

Ananya looked at him properly for the first time since he'd walked in, and felt something cold move through her that Ankush's shouting, for all its volume, had entirely failed to produce. Ankush's anger, whatever else it was, was at least honestly itself - a boy humiliated in public, saying so badly and loudly. Niraj's calm wasn't honest. It sat on him too well, too practised, a man who had learned somewhere along the way that the quietest voice in a room was very often the one doing the most damage, and meant every unhurried word of it.

"It won't happen again," Suraj said. He said it to Niraj, not to Ananya, his eyes not quite meeting his daughter's as the sentence left him, and Ananya heard, underneath the flat, reasonable delivery of it, a man saying something he had already decided, privately, not to make true. "Ananya understands the position this puts the family in. There's no cause for either of you to worry further."

"I'm glad to hear it." Niraj's smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Ankush is fond of her, whatever this evening might suggest to the contrary. It would be a shame for a foolish morning to undo what our families have already agreed to." A small pause, weighted with exactly as much as he intended it to carry. "Especially given how much Wolverine Industries has already put behind Vikas Limited's recovery. I'd hate for anyone to think that goodwill was misplaced."

Suraj's face didn't change. Ananya, watching him as closely as she'd watched anyone in her life, saw the words land anyway - not on his expression, somewhere further in, a small settling motion like a man absorbing a blow he'd known, in some form, was always going to arrive eventually.

"It isn't misplaced," Suraj said.

"Good." Niraj's hand found his son's shoulder, steering him gently, almost paternally, toward the door. "Then we needn't speak of this again. Come, beta. We've taken enough of the evening."

Ankush went, though not without one last look back at Ananya from the doorway - not the fury he'd walked in with, something rawer underneath it now that he hadn't quite managed to hide before he turned away, gone before she could decide what exactly she'd seen. The door closed behind them both. The flat, ordinary sound of the latch settling seemed, for a moment, louder than anything either of them had said all evening.

Sandhya let out a breath she'd clearly been holding since the bell rang, and murmured something about checking on dinner that nobody in the room believed for a second was actually about dinner - but not before her eyes went, briefly, to Suraj, at the exact word *misplaced*, with a look Ananya had seen on her mother's face perhaps twice before in her life: not understanding, not even close to it, but the specific unease of a woman who has just heard her husband answer a question she didn't know had been asked. She left them alone in the front room in the particular silence that follows a scene rather than a conversation.

"Papa." Ananya waited until she was certain her voice would hold before she used it. "Are you really going to marry me to him?"

Suraj didn't answer straight away. He crossed to the window instead, looking out at nothing in particular, his back to her for long enough that she began to wonder whether he intended to answer at all.

"Papa."

"Go and change," he said, finally, not unkindly, not answering either. "It's been a long day for all of us."

She stood there a moment longer, waiting for something more that didn't come, and understood, the way she'd understood a great many things about her father over twenty-five years without ever once needing him to say them aloud, that this particular silence wasn't the silence of a man who hadn't heard the question. It was the silence of a man who knew exactly what his answer was, and had not yet decided he was ready to let her hear it.

She went to change, as he'd asked, and lay awake a long while afterward listening to the house settle around her - her mother's footsteps in the kitchen long after any dinner needed making, her father's study light still on well past the hour he usually gave it up for the night. She thought of Niraj's voice, unhurried and pleasant even as it said the thing it had actually come to say, and found that of everything spoken in that room that evening, it was his calm rather than Ankush's shouting that she kept returning to in the dark - the specific, settled quiet of a man who never once had to raise his voice to make himself understood.
