# Chapter Twelve: I Was Wrong

Sanya came back three evenings later, without the phone this time, without the urgency either - just herself, at Ananya's door, holding a small box of the sweets from the place near her office that Ananya had always liked and Sanya never quite remembered to buy unprompted.

"I owe you something," she said, before Ananya had even properly stepped back to let her in. "I've been rehearsing it the whole drive over, so let me just say it before I lose my nerve."

"You don't have to rehearse an apology to me."

"I do, actually. You'll see why." Sanya set the box down on the kitchen counter and didn't sit, the way she never sat for anything she considered genuinely important, as if standing kept the words more honest. "I was cruel to you. Not on purpose - I want to be clear I never meant it cruelly - but I sat on that library bench six years ago and told you, with total confidence, that you were building your whole future on nothing. I was wrong about the nothing. I think I owe you an apology for that specifically, not just a - " she gestured, vaguely, at the general shape of the last few days - "not just a congratulations."

Ananya leaned against the counter, watching her with the particular patient warmth she'd always reserved for Sanya at her most carefully organised. "Go on, then. I'm listening."

"There's a part I never told you." Sanya turned her cup a quarter-turn on the counter, not drinking from it, the same restless habit she'd carried since college. "Priya's cousin's engagement, three years ago. The boy I introduced you to that evening, the one who worked in logistics."

"Vishal."

"Vishal." Sanya winced slightly, saying the name back. "That wasn't a coincidence, Ananya. I asked his sister to seat him next to you. I told myself I was just being a good friend, widening your options, but that's not what it was. I wanted you to have someone standing in front of you, someone real, so you'd see how thin a six-year-old dream looked next to an actual man asking for your number." She exhaled. "I did it twice more after that, smaller ways. I never once told you, because saying it out loud would have meant admitting I didn't trust you to know your own mind."

"I noticed," Ananya said, mildly, and something in Sanya's face went still. "Not the sister part. But I noticed you kept finding reasons to put men in front of me that year. I let it go because I understood what you were actually worried about, even if you never said it plainly."

"You let it go for *three years*."

"You're my friend. You were scared for me, not against me. I didn't see the point in making you say the quiet part out loud before you were ready to." Ananya reached for the sweets box, if only to have something to do with her hands. "I'm glad you said it now, though. I'd rather have it named than carried."

Sanya sat, finally, dropping into the chair at the small kitchen table like the standing had cost her something to maintain. "I've known you since we were nine years old," she said, "and I still doubted you completely over something that turned out to be entirely true. That should embarrass me more than it apparently does."

"For what it's worth," Ananya said, "I never held it against you. Not once. You said the true and reasonable things a true and reasonable person would say to a friend who told them a Goddess visited her in a dream. I'd have said the same, if our places were reversed."

"Would you?"

"Probably not as gently as you did," Ananya admitted, and something in Sanya's face eased at that, a small honest laugh escaping despite the seriousness she'd walked in with.

---

"I want to be clear about one more thing," Sanya said, once the tea had been made and the sweets finally opened between them, back on steadier, more familiar ground. "I'm not suddenly a believer. I saw a photograph of a man who used to be disabled and now isn't, and I'll grant you that's remarkable, genuinely. But a Goddess telling you to marry him in a dream - I still don't know what to do with that part. I don't think I ever will, honestly, and I don't think you need me to."

"I don't."

"Good. Because I was going to say it anyway." Sanya smiled, properly now, the old teasing warmth back in her voice for the first time since she'd walked through the door. "I believe *you*. I believe something happened to you that night, and I believe you've been right about the parts of it that could actually be checked. The Goddess herself can stay between you and whatever she is. I'm not signing up to explain her to anyone."

"That's fair."

"It's the most I've got. I hope it's enough."

"It's more than enough," Ananya said, and meant it, reaching across the small table to take her hand the way she had a hundred times before, over fifteen years of exactly this kind of conversation - not the last one they'd ever have about Shaktiyuth, she suspected, but the last one that needed an apology attached to it. "I was never keeping score, Sanya. I promise you that. I just needed you to still be here on the other side of it, however long it took."

"I was always going to be here."

"I know," Ananya said. "That's the part I never doubted either."
