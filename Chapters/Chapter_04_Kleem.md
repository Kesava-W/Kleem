# Chapter Four: Kleem

By the end of the first month, Kleeba had stopped consulting the list Revati had left her - the warmed milk, the particular fold of the blanket, the hour he liked the curtains drawn rather than the hour the sun actually left the room - because she no longer needed a list to know these things. She simply knew them, the way you know the layout of a house you've lived in a long time, by having walked through it often enough that your feet stopped needing your eyes.

He was not, in those early months, an easy child to read. He cried the way other children cried, but for reasons that took Kleeba longer than she expected to learn - not hunger, not always tiredness, sometimes nothing she could name at all except that some small thing in his morning had gone the wrong shape and he had no way yet to tell her which small thing it was. She learned to watch his hands instead of his face. His hands told her first, every time - fists before the crying started, fingers working at nothing, a full half-minute of warning if she was paying attention.

She was almost always paying attention. It was, she had told Revati at that first interview, the one thing she knew she was good at.

Her own sister, hearing what the job actually involved, had asked her once, not unkindly, whether it wasn't exhausting - watching a child so closely, all day, for signs he couldn't yet put into words himself. Kleeba had thought about it and found the honest answer wasn't the one her sister expected. It wasn't exhausting so much as it was absorbing, in the way close work always was - the same patience a tailor gave a difficult seam, or a farmer gave a field that wouldn't take the seed the easy way. You didn't call that exhausting. You called it the job, and you did it properly or you didn't do it at all.

---

He was two and a half when he first tried her name.

She had said it to him a thousand times by then, the way you say your own name to a child without expecting anything back for a long while yet - *Kleeba, beta, Kleeba's here* - until the morning he looked up from the wooden blocks he'd been failing, patiently, to stack for the better part of ten minutes, and said, with the full seriousness of a much older child announcing something important:

"Kleem."

Kleeba laughed before she could stop herself - not unkindly, just surprised, the sound escaping before she'd decided whether surprise was the right response. He didn't seem to mind the laugh. He said it again, pleased with himself in the specific, uncomplicated way he was pleased with very little else that month, and went back to the blocks as though the matter were settled.

"Kleeba," she corrected, gently, the way she corrected everything, without urgency, the same tone whether he'd said her name wrong or knocked over his milk.

"Kleem," he said, and this time it sounded less like a mistake and more like a decision.

She corrected him for a week. Then two. By the third week she had stopped expecting the correction to take, not out of any particular worry - children got things wrong for longer than this, plenty of them, and came right eventually - but simply because "Kleem" had, somewhere in that fortnight, stopped sounding like an error to her at all and started sounding like a name. His name for her. She answered to it without thinking, the way you'd answer to a nickname a sibling gave you as a child and never quite grew out of.

She mentioned it to Revati once, in passing, expecting nothing more than the small parental amusement such things usually got. "He calls me Kleem now. Can't get his mouth round the rest of it."

Revati smiled the tired, fond smile of a mother hearing something small and sweet about her son at the end of a long day, and said something about how Saketh's own nephew hadn't managed "grandmother" properly until he was nearly five, and neither of them thought about it again. Why would they have. It was, by every measure either of them had, an entirely ordinary thing for a slow, quiet boy to get wrong.

---

The years that followed did not move the way years were supposed to move for a child his age.

Other things stayed slow in the way the doctors had warned they might. He was seven before he tied his own shoes without help, later than the other children in the colony, later even than the books allowed for. He held a pencil like something he was fighting rather than using, the letters coming out lopsided and enormous long after his classmates' had shrunk to a manageable size. He struggled, badly, with anything that asked him to take what he'd learned in one place and use it somewhere else - counting the wooden blocks perfectly, every time, and then standing baffled in front of a shop counter when Kleeba asked him to count out coins the same way. When his routine broke - the wrong cup at breakfast, a favourite door left open that should have been shut - he did not always have words for what was wrong, and on the bad days he went somewhere quiet inside himself instead, sitting very still, saying nothing, until whatever had upset him had run its course on its own schedule rather than anyone else's.

He did not play the games the other children played. He watched them, mostly, from the edge of the garden wall or the school compound, without the particular unhappiness Kleeba had half-expected to see there - he didn't seem to mind being outside the game so much as he simply didn't understand, most days, how one joined it.

And yet.

There was the afternoon, when he was nine, that Kleeba came into the front room to find every photograph on the wall shifted a careful half-inch from where it usually hung, and Shaktiyuth sitting cross-legged beneath them, deeply satisfied, having apparently spent the better part of an hour noticing - correctly, in every case - that the frames had been hung very slightly crooked for as long as he could remember, and fixing every one.

There was the drawer of jigsaw puzzles Saketh had bought him one Diwali, meant for a child two years younger, that Shaktiyuth finished so quickly and so completely without ever once looking at the box's picture that Saketh eventually stopped buying them for his age and started buying them, without telling anyone why, for adults instead - puzzles meant to take a grown man an evening, that Shaktiyuth put together, silently, methodically, in under an hour, and never once wanted to talk about afterward, as though the doing of it were the whole of the reward.

There was the badminton racket, too, the summer he was nine - not his own idea; one of the neighbourhood boys had left it leaning against the compound wall and gone in for water, and Shaktiyuth had picked it up, turned it over twice in his hands the way he turned everything over before deciding what it was for, and hit the shuttle clean over the wall on his very first proper swing, to nobody's astonishment more than his own. He didn't pick it up again for another two years. When he finally did, he remembered the exact angle of that first swing without anyone having to show him twice - which was, Kleeba thought, watching him from the veranda, very much like everything else about him: the useful things arrived early and then waited, patiently, for the rest of him to be ready to use them.

Kleeba noticed these things and said nothing to anyone in particular about them, not because she thought they meant something - she didn't, not then, not with any words she could have put to it - but because she had learned, in her years with him, that Shaktiyuth's gifts arrived exactly like his difficulties did: quietly, sideways, in places nobody had thought to look.

---

The worst of the bad days came when he was ten, a Tuesday that started wrong somewhere Kleeba never fully identified and got no better as it went - a meltdown at breakfast, a ruined shirt, a walk to school abandoned halfway when he simply stopped and would not move, standing in the middle of the lane with his fists closed and his eyes fixed on nothing, while Kleeba crouched in front of him and said his name, his real one, over and over, in the voice she used for exactly this.

He came back to himself slowly, the way he always did, in his own time and not a second before it. When he did, he didn't apologise - he never quite had the words for that either, not at that age - but he reached out, unprompted, and took her hand, the whole walk back to the house, holding it the way a much younger child might, and didn't let go until they were through the gate.

"Kleem," he said, quietly, once they were inside, not as a mistake and not quite as a name either - something closer, Kleeba thought, though she couldn't have explained why she thought it, to a kind of thank you.

"I know, beta," she said, and made him his tea the way he liked it, and didn't mention the morning again.

She would remember that walk for a long time afterward, longer than she remembered most of the good days. Not because anything about it had been remarkable to look at - a boy who couldn't yet say sorry, holding the hand of the one person in his life who had never once needed him to.
