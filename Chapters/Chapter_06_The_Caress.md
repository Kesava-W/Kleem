# Chapter Six: The Caress

The house kept its own kind of evening quiet, the sort that belonged to a home where the people who lived in it worked hard and came back late and had long ago stopped apologising to each other for it. The garden gate was shut. The guava tree threw its usual crooked shadow across the veranda, longer now that the sun had gone. Somewhere past the boundary wall a dog barked twice at nothing and gave it up.

Inside, the house was dark except for one low lamp left burning out of habit rather than need. The kitchen still smelled faintly of the evening's dal, the plates already washed and stacked, the day's ordinary chores folded away the way Kleeba always left them before she allowed herself to sit down at all.

Kleeba had fallen asleep in the chair beside his bed, the way she sometimes did on the evenings Saketh and Revati ran late - not from any particular tiredness that night, just the ordinary drift of a long day settling into her all at once, her chin dropped to her chest, her hands still loosely folded in her lap the way they'd been when she'd meant only to close her eyes a moment.

Shaktiyuth slept the way he usually slept - on his side, one hand curled near his face, his breathing even and untroubled. Eleven years old, and small for it, the room around him arranged the particular way it always was: the jigsaw puzzles stacked by size on the shelf, the photographs on the wall hung exactly straight, the door left open the width he preferred it, no more, no less.

It was, in every visible way, one more night in a life made almost entirely of nights like it - a boy who slept easily and a woman who had learned to sleep lightly beside him, in a house that had long ago made its peace with a slower, more careful kind of ordinary than the one most houses ran on. Nothing in the room announced that this night would be different from the thousand nights before it.

She came without sound, the way she always did, and without any door having opened to let her in. Light arrived with her rather than around her - not enough to wake either of the room's two sleepers, not enough to throw a shadow, just enough that anyone watching would have known, without needing to be told, that this was not an ordinary visitor.

She did not wake Kleeba. She did not disturb the lamp, or the puzzles, or the careful straightness of the frames on the wall. She crossed the small room the way you cross a room you already know completely, and sat on the edge of the bed beside the sleeping boy, unhurried, as though she had all the time there was and had always intended to spend some of it exactly here.

For a while she only looked at him.

Then she reached out and touched his hair, once - not a caress that woke him, not one that stirred him even in his sleep, just a hand passing gently over dark, unremarkable hair the way you'd touch something you had already decided was yours to be gentle with. She smiled while she did it. Not the smile of someone beginning something. The smile, if anyone had been awake to read it rightly, of someone finishing something that had already been a very long time in the making.

She stayed a moment longer than the touch itself required. Then she rose, unhurried as she'd sat, and crossed back the way she'd come, and the room was dark again in the ordinary way, the lamp burning the same low, habitual light it had burned all evening, Kleeba still asleep in her chair with her hands still folded in her lap.

Shaktiyuth did not wake. He did not stir, or dream differently than he had been dreaming, or give any sign at all, sleeping there in the dark, that anything in the room had changed.

Nothing had - not yet, not that anyone could have pointed to. The puzzles were still stacked by size. The frames still hung exactly straight. When Saketh's car pulled into the drive twenty minutes later, headlights sweeping briefly across the ceiling, Kleeba woke with a start, glanced at the boy still sleeping soundly beside her, and thought nothing more of it than that it had, on the whole, been an unremarkable evening.

It would be some weeks yet before anyone in that house understood that it hadn't been.
