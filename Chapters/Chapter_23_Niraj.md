# Chapter Twenty-Three: Niraj

They found Ankush on the path down, where the water had left him, the wreck of all three cars and the men who'd been in them scattered further along the mud the flood had carved out of the slope.

Shaktiyuth didn't slow the car. He kept his eyes forward, one hand briefly finding Ananya's before the headlights swept past the shapes at the path's edge, and neither of them said anything about it, then or for a long time afterward. There would be a place, eventually, for what that sight had actually meant. It was not this road, and it was not tonight.

The drive back to the city passed in a silence neither of them tried to fill. Ananya sat with both hands folded in her lap, her face turned toward the window and the dark trees sliding past it, her breathing gone slow and deliberate in the particular way of someone working very hard to keep it that way. Shaktiyuth let her have the silence, understanding that whatever she needed to do with it was hers to do without him narrating the night back to her while she did it. He did not know, watching the road instead of her, whether she was thinking about Ankush, or about her father, or about nothing at all yet, the mind's own small mercy after more than it could process in one night. He didn't ask. There would be time for that too, later, when *later* had stopped meaning anything as urgent as it did right now.

The apartment's gate stood open when they reached it, exactly as Suraj had left it hours before, and beyond it, in the drive, a second car Shaktiyuth didn't recognise was parked at an angle that suggested it had arrived in some hurry.

Niraj was waiting by the door.

He came down the steps before the car had fully stopped, already searching the back seat with his eyes before either door opened, and Shaktiyuth understood, watching him cross the last of the distance, that this was a man who had spent the last hour telling himself increasingly desperate stories about why his phone calls weren't being answered.

"Where is he." Niraj's voice had none of its usual composure left in it, not even the thin coat of it he'd worn at the confrontation weeks before. "Where is my son."

Shaktiyuth got out of the car slowly, positioning himself between Niraj and Ananya without appearing to, and looked at the older man for a long moment before he answered.

"You should be grateful," he said, quietly, "if you find him alive."

Something went out of Niraj's face when he heard it - not understanding, not yet, just the first crack running through whatever certainty had been holding him upright until that sentence. "What does that mean. What did you do to him."

"I did nothing to him he didn't bring on himself, coming after us with men and cars in the middle of the night." Shaktiyuth's voice stayed even, unhurried, the same register he'd used to correct a wrong figure a hundred times before tonight and would likely use to correct a hundred more. "I gave him a chance to turn back on his own path. He chose not to take it."

"That isn't an answer." Niraj's hands had begun to shake, visibly, the first genuine loss of composure Shaktiyuth had seen in him since they'd first stood close enough to read each other properly. "Where is my son. Tell me plainly, or so help me -"

"Go and look," Shaktiyuth said. "I won't stop you. I'd rather you heard it from the road itself than from me."

Niraj didn't move. Something in him seemed to already know, standing there in the drive with his hands shaking and his breath coming too fast, exactly what waiting at the end of that road would look like, and some last, thin thread of denial kept his feet planted where they were rather than let him go and confirm it.

Suraj had come out onto the step by then, drawn by the voices, and stood there without moving to intervene, watching the scene with the particular stillness of a man seeing the last part of a calculation he'd made weeks ago finally arrive at its answer.

Niraj's composure, held through every room he'd walked into since this story began - the confident shareholder, the courteous rival, the man who had never once needed to raise his voice to be understood - simply gave way. It didn't crumble slowly. It went all at once, the way a wall goes once the one weak seam in it finally reaches its limit, and what was left standing in its place bore almost no resemblance to the man who had stood so calmly in Suraj's front room only weeks before.

"He's my son." Niraj's voice had gone high and cracked, nothing composed left in it anywhere. "He's my only son, and you - " He stopped, both hands coming up to his own head as though he could physically hold the thought in by force. "I did this. I did this to my own family, the same as I -"

He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't need to. Shaktiyuth watched him arrive at whatever he'd been circling for the last several seconds and simply waited, saying nothing, giving him the room to say it himself rather than dragging it out of him.

"I poisoned her," Niraj said. The words came out of him in a rush now, unstoppable, a dam that had held for twenty-seven years finally giving up the last of its resistance. "Revati. Your mother. A sweet, at a function, years before you were born - I made it myself, in my own kitchen, like a coward too proud to buy something that could be traced back to a shop. Saketh and I were friends, we built everything together in those years, and then his company started outpacing mine and I couldn't - I couldn't stand it, watching him win at the one thing I'd always thought I was better at than him. I told myself it would only make her ill for a while. Enough to slow them down. Enough to give me room to catch up." His breath caught, ragged. "I never meant for what it did to you. I told myself that for twenty-seven years, every single one of them. I'm not sure I ever believed it, not really, not even on the days I managed to say it out loud to my own reflection."

The night went very quiet around the confession, the ordinary sounds of the street somehow further away than they'd been a moment ago. Suraj, on the step, had gone rigid in a way Shaktiyuth recognised without needing to be told what it meant - a man learning, in a single sentence, why an old business rival had made himself so easy a target for exactly the arrangement that had bought him a stake in Vikas Limited.

Shaktiyuth said nothing for a long moment.

"I know," he said, finally.

Niraj stared at him. "You - what?"

"I've known for a long time," Shaktiyuth said, and there was nothing in his voice that resembled triumph, only a very old, very settled fact being stated plainly for the first time to someone other than himself. "Longer than I could easily tell you the beginning of."

"You *knew.*" Something in Niraj seemed to break a second time, differently from the first, the ground shifting under a grief that had apparently been standing, this whole time, on the assumption that his secret was still his own to carry. "How. How could you possibly have - I told no one. Not my wife, not Ankush, not a single living soul in twenty-seven years." He said it almost to himself now, working through it aloud the way a man argues with an empty room. "Someone must have told you. A servant. One of the old staff, if any of them ever suspected - or Saketh himself, if he worked it out and never once let on that he had, all these years, letting me believe -" He stopped, shook his head, abandoning that thread as fast as he'd picked it up. "No. He'd have said something. He was never that good at silence, even when it would have served him."

He stared at Shaktiyuth as though seeing something genuinely inhuman for the first time, more frightened by this than by anything else the night had shown him. "There was a function, four or five months ago. Someone mentioned your name - your rise, your businesses, all of it - and I felt something go through me I couldn't explain even to myself afterward, something closer to dread than surprise, as though some part of me already knew before the rest of me had caught up to why. I told myself it was nothing. I have spent twenty-seven years telling myself a great many things were nothing." His voice cracked properly now, for the first time. "You've known all this time, and you never once came to me. Never wrote, never confronted me, never let me see it in your face at that function or any other. You let me go on believing I was safe."

"It was never mine to settle with words," Shaktiyuth said. "Only to decide what I was going to do about it. I decided a long time ago." He didn't elaborate further, and something in his stillness made it clear he wasn't going to be asked to.

Niraj went down onto his knees in the gravel, all at once, both hands pressed flat against his own face. "Then finish it," he said, muffled, and looked up with an expression Shaktiyuth had genuinely never seen on another human being before that moment - not fear of death, something closer to a plea for it. "Kill me. I've earned it twice over tonight. Whatever you did to my son, do it to me too, and let this be finished."

"No," Shaktiyuth said.

"Why not." It came out of Niraj almost as a scream. "You have every right -"

"Because it isn't my job," Shaktiyuth said, quietly, and there was nothing cold in the way he said it, only a plain, immovable fact, the same register he'd have used to decline any task that simply wasn't his to perform. "What happens to you now isn't mine to decide either. I only ever came here for her."

Niraj made a sound then that wasn't quite a word - something closer to the sound a much smaller thing makes when the last of its strength finally leaves it - and folded forward over his own knees, weeping for his son in great, ragged, unashamed sobs that filled the whole quiet street, still begging, between them, for someone, anyone, to simply end it for him.

He stopped, quite suddenly, mid-sentence.

He did not start again.

Shaktiyuth crossed the few remaining steps and crouched beside him, checked, and found nothing left to check for. He straightened slowly, without ceremony, and looked toward Suraj, who had already come down off the step, his face unreadable in the security light.

Suraj looked down at Niraj's still shape a moment longer than the situation strictly required, and said, quietly, mostly to himself, "So that's what my company was actually worth to you." He didn't wait for anyone to respond to it. Whatever he'd needed the sentence to do, saying it once seemed to be enough.

"The police will come eventually, whatever we tell them," he said, low and fast, already moving. "Go. Both of you. Now, before anyone else in this street wakes to see what's lying in my driveway."

"Papa -" Ananya had come to stand beside them at some point Shaktiyuth hadn't fully registered, staring down at the still shape in the gravel with an expression that hadn't yet decided what it was.

"There isn't time," Suraj said, gently, and it was the gentleness, more than the urgency, that finally moved her. "Go. I'll manage what's left here. I always do."

Shaktiyuth took her hand. They went, out through the gate and into a street just beginning, at its furthest edge, to grey with the first suggestion of morning, and left Suraj standing alone in his own driveway with a debt finally, entirely paid and no one left to tell him so.
