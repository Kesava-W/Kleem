# Chapter Twenty-Two: The Water Tank

The forest road ran out of streetlight eight minutes past the city's last checkpost, and after that there was only the car's own headlights and the dark pressing close on both sides, trees crowding the tarmac the way a held breath crowds a chest. The radio, still playing something soft and forgettable from whenever Suraj had last had the car serviced, felt suddenly obscene against the silence underneath it, and Ananya reached over without a word and switched it off.

Ananya sat with one hand still gripping his sleeve, not out of fear exactly, more the simple need to keep some part of him within reach after a night that had already taken so much on faith. Shaktiyuth kept both hands on the wheel and most of his attention on the mirror behind them, where a pair of headlights had appeared four minutes ago, fallen back once when the road bent, and reappeared closer than before.

"We could keep driving," she said, once, testing the idea rather than proposing it. "Further into the city. Somewhere with people."

"They'd catch us before the city." He said it plainly, without alarm, the same register he'd have used to correct a wrong figure on a balance sheet. "This car isn't built for that kind of road, and theirs will have been chosen because it is. We need ground that helps us more than it helps them. Not speed."

She didn't ask what that meant yet. He was glad of it. He wasn't entirely sure himself what shape the answer would take until the answer showed itself to him, the way answers to this particular kind of question always eventually did - not reasoned toward, exactly, more recognised, the instant the right shape crossed his path.

"They're following," she said.

"Yes."

"How many?"

"Three cars." He didn't need to check the mirror again to know it. Something in the rhythm of the lights behind them - a half-second later at every bend, drivers less familiar with this road than the one ahead of them - had already told him more than a clearer look would have. "Perhaps twelve men, all told, counting Ankush."

She didn't ask how he knew that either. There would be time, later, for all the questions she was visibly saving up behind a face gone very still and very determined in the dashboard's dim light. Right now she simply nodded, once, and held on.

He saw the tank before the road gave any reason to expect it - a pale, elevated shape through a gap in the trees, old stone gone grey-green with age, standing on a rise above a single narrow path that climbed to it and nothing else. He didn't decide to take the turning so much as recognise it, the way you recognise the one right answer in a page of wrong ones a half-second before you could have explained your reasoning: high ground, a single approach, water. He took the turning.

The path climbed rough and rutted, the car complaining the whole way up, until it opened onto a flat shelf of cleared ground beside the tank itself - enormous up close, a squat stone drum wider across than the house he'd grown up in, its walls climbing higher than a man's reach, one narrow iron ladder bolted up its near side toward a rusted inspection hatch at the top. He parked at an angle, nose toward the path they'd come up, and was out of the car before Ananya had finished asking what he was doing.

"Stay here."

"Shaktiyuth -"

"Please, Anu." He said it quietly, already moving toward the tank's base. "I need both hands free, and I need to know exactly where you are while I use them."

She stayed, for as long as staying was a thing she could manage from inside a car with the engine off and nothing to do but watch. He felt her watching from the car the whole time he crossed the last stretch of open ground, and set the feeling gently aside, the way he set aside anything that wasn't the actual problem in front of him.

He walked the tank's circumference once, slow, one palm trailing the stone. Old mortar. A century of monsoons soaking in and drying out, soaking in and drying out, the same slow violence repeated ten thousand times until something in the structure had quietly stopped being as sound as it looked. He'd done this before, in a smaller way, more times than he could count and without ever once naming it to himself as a method - stood in front of a system, a spreadsheet, a stranger's face, and simply waited for the one wrong note in it to make itself known against everything around it that was still humming true. This was the same listening, applied to stone instead of numbers. He trusted it exactly as much here as he trusted it anywhere else, which was to say completely, and without needing to understand why it worked.

He found what he was looking for on the side facing the ascending path - a hairline seam, darker than the stone around it, a faint outward bulge no wider than his hand where the wall had begun, years ago, to lose an argument with the water it held. He didn't need to think about it any longer than that. He simply knew, the way he'd known Ananya's name on nothing but a feeling in a garden months ago, that this was the place.

He searched the cleared ground until he found a stone the right size - not large, but dense, heavy enough to matter, small enough that his hand could still close properly around it. He carried it back to the seam and started hitting.

The first strike did nothing he could see.

The second did nothing either.

He kept going. His shoulder began complaining after the twentieth strike, a dull ache spreading down toward his elbow that had nothing paranormal about it at all - this part of the work, whatever else was true about the rest of him, was simply a man's arm doing a hard, repetitive thing until it hurt. Sweat ran into his eyes. He didn't stop to wipe it.

Behind him, the car door opened - Ananya, unable to sit still inside a locked box a moment longer, stepping out into the open to see better what he was doing - and the small interior light came on with it, a single yellow flare against all that dark before she caught the mistake and pushed the door shut again. It was gone in under two seconds. It was also, Shaktiyuth understood without turning around, precisely the kind of thing a driver scanning the treeline for exactly this sort of light would not need more than two seconds to notice. He didn't call out to her. There wasn't time to spend on a mistake already made, only on finishing before whatever it had cost them came due.

He stopped once, briefly, not from doubt but to breathe, and in that one still moment heard, with sudden clarity, everything the effort had been drowning out - his own pulse, the water still weeping its thin thread down the stone, and, distinct now over the rise, more engine noise joining the first. The rest of them. Closing.

He picked the stone back up.

*Crack.*

A hairline became a visible one. He hit it again, harder, both hands on the stone now.

*Crack.*

The visible line lengthened, a thin dark thread running upward through the old mortar, water beginning to weep from it in a thread no wider than a shoelace.

He heard the cars before he saw them - three engines, straining up the rutted path, headlights swinging wild across the trees as the ground fought them the whole way. A voice carried up ahead of them, distorted by distance and the engine noise into something that was recognisably Ankush's without being any of his actual words. Shaktiyuth didn't stop to make out what he was shouting. He hit the seam once more, as hard as he had ever hit anything in his life, and felt something in the wall finally, fully give.

He moved.

He was three strides clear when the crack became a mouth. The sound arrived a half-second behind the sight of it - not a roar, not at first, something closer to a vast intake of breath before the wall simply stopped being a wall in that one place, stone and mortar punching outward in a spray of grey fragments, and behind it a wall of water that had been waiting a hundred years for exactly this permission.

It came down the path the way water comes down anything that used to be dry - fast, indifferent, filling every low place at once. The first cars were already on the ascending path when it hit, headlights swinging up and sideways as the ground beneath them simply stopped holding still. Shaktiyuth heard shouting, cut off. He heard metal against stone, more than once. He did not watch closely enough to describe, afterward, exactly what happened to any of them, and found, in the moment itself, that he had no wish to.

Then, almost as fast as it had begun, it was over. The tank emptied itself in under a minute, the roar dropping to a rush and then to a trickle and then to the ordinary sound of water finding its own level in the dark, and the sudden quiet afterward was its own kind of violence, a silence with too much weight behind it to feel like relief.

He stood at the tank's ruined base a moment, breathing hard, hands scraped and stinging, and did not go closer yet to see what the water had left along the path below.

In the stillness, unbidden, a memory arrived that had nothing to do with any of this - a lane near his own childhood house, a Tuesday gone wrong in some way he'd never fully understood even at the time, his own small fists closed around nothing, and a hand finding his and simply holding it, the whole walk home, without once asking him to explain himself. *I know, beta.* He had not thought of that walk in years. He did not know, standing here with his own hands bleeding faintly in the dark, why it had chosen this exact moment to return to him, except that it was, perhaps, the oldest example he had of a thing being done and never once needing to be spoken about afterward.

He looked back toward the car. Ananya was out of it properly now, standing a careful distance away with both arms wrapped around herself, watching him with an expression that held more questions than fear now, though it held plenty of both.

"Is it done?" she asked, when he was close enough to hear her without raising her voice.

"It's done," he said.

She didn't ask him what *done* had actually cost, not yet, not standing here with the night still ringing faintly in both their ears. She simply took his hand - the scraped one, gently, as though she already understood without being told that it was the one that needed it - and turned back toward the car.

They still had to go back the way they'd come. Neither of them said so aloud. Both of them, walking to the car in the returning quiet, understood exactly what waited for them somewhere along that path, and neither hurried toward finding out.
