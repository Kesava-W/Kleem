# Chapter Nine: Five Years

Graduation came and went with less ceremony than any of them had expected of it - a stage, a handshake, a photograph nobody quite smiled properly for because all four of them were already thinking about Monday.

Revati's land sat two hours outside the city, more of it than any of them had known what to do with at first: fallow acres that had belonged to her own father, farmed halfheartedly by tenants for years before she'd finally asked for it back. She split it four ways without much ceremony either. Vikram got his corner for the toy manufacturing line he'd been sketching on napkins since second year. Devansh got his for the hardware unit. Sujay got his for the healthcare equipment line that had started, quietly, as a tribute to a grandmother who never did find out her grandson had built anything at all because of her. And Shaktiyuth kept the largest share for himself - not because he needed the most, but because what he had in mind needed room to fail in before it could work.

The first eighteen months were harder on all three of the others than any of them admitted to each other outright. Vikram's first production run came back with a manufacturing defect that cost him nearly everything he'd put in, and he spent a week not answering anyone's calls before he reappeared, sketching a fix on the back of the same defective batch's packing slip, entirely undiscouraged in the way that was, Shaktiyuth had come to understand, simply how Vikram grieved. Devansh's hardware unit failed two safety certifications before it passed a third, each failure catalogued and cross-referenced in a spreadsheet he refused to let anyone else see. Sujay's line found buyers slower than either of the other two, small hospitals wary of an unproven vendor, and there were months Shaktiyuth quietly covered a shortfall in his corner of the shared land lease without either of them ever discussing it as a loan.

---

What he had in mind, when he'd first explained it to his parents, was two ventures wearing the shape of one piece of land: dairy on the near half, cattle sheds and a milking parlour he built himself out of a manual he'd read twice and understood on the first pass; crops on the far half, rotated the way his neighbours rotated theirs, because he'd decided early on that there was no version of being new to farming that skipped the part where you actually learned it. The software company was a separate thing entirely - a small office two hours away in the city, three hired engineers by the end of the first year, work that had nothing to do with cattle or crops at all.

The farm humbled him faster than the software ever had. He understood systems - had understood them, in one form or another, since long before he had words for what understanding them meant - but a herd of cattle was not a system that rewarded being right quickly. A cow either calved well or didn't, regardless of how cleanly he'd modelled the odds. A field either took the season's rain or it didn't, and no amount of overnight cleverness moved a monsoon. He was, by his own private admission, a mediocre farmer for the better part of a year, patient with the failures in a way he hadn't always managed to be patient with himself as a boy - because a boy who had once needed the long road himself was not, it turned out, the kind of man who resented a slow field for needing one too.

The software company was the opposite problem entirely: not too slow, but too easy to be trusted at first, three young engineers with more confidence than clients and a founder more comfortable debugging their code at two in the morning than pitching it to anyone by day.

---

It was a neighbouring farmer, Ramulu, who did more than anyone to fix the first problem, and the hospital board from the contest who unknowingly fixed the second.

Ramulu had worked the land two plots over for longer than Shaktiyuth had been alive, sharper than most people gave him credit for and skeptical of exactly nothing except things that hadn't yet earned his skepticism. He watched the new dairy-and-crop operation fail quietly through its first monsoon, said nothing for the better part of that year, and then, unprompted, turned up one morning with a pair of hands and no explanation beyond, "You're doing the drainage wrong. It'll cost you the whole east field by August."

It did not cost him the whole east field. Ramulu made sure of that, the way he made sure of most things on that stretch of land - by simply showing up, again and again, through a second year and a third, correcting things Shaktiyuth hadn't known to ask about, never once accepting payment for it beyond the tea Shaktiyuth learned to have ready.

"Why do you keep coming," Shaktiyuth finally asked him, well into the second year, "when I've nothing to offer you for it?"

"Because you keep at it properly," Ramulu said, "instead of giving it up for something easier, the way most city boys would have by now." It was, Shaktiyuth understood, as close to a compliment as the man gave anyone, and he didn't waste it by thanking him for it out loud.

The software side turned the same year, for a plainer reason: the hospital board that had judged the contest, remembering the system they'd asked to keep a working copy of, came back with an actual contract once Sujay's monitors were already installed in their wing - a small piece of work at first, then a larger one, then, by the third year, a standing relationship that brought two more hospitals through the same door without Shaktiyuth having pitched either of them directly.

---

The five years that followed moved faster than the first had, the way things generally do once the hardest part is behind them. Vikram's toy line found a distributor who took it national within eighteen months. Devansh's hardware unit landed a contract that dwarfed anything any of them had built in college. Sujay's healthcare equipment found its way, eventually, into the same category of hospital board that had once judged their contest - a small, private satisfaction none of them mentioned aloud, though all four of them noticed it.

Both ventures grew steadily from there, in their own separate rhythms. The dairy-and-crop operation never made him rich on its own - it wasn't built to, and he'd never asked it to be - but by the fifth year it ran clean and reliable, the kind of farm neighbours pointed to without needing to explain why. The software company grew faster and louder, eleven engineers by then, the hospital contracts joined by two more outside healthcare entirely, a business that had, somewhere in its third year, stopped needing him at the centre of every decision and started running the way well-built systems were supposed to run: mostly on their own, checked rather than carried.

A trade paper ran a short piece on the farm in year four, more interested in Ramulu's drainage fix than anything Shaktiyuth had done, which suited him entirely fine. He read it once, noted they'd gotten the east field's size slightly wrong, considered writing in to correct it, and didn't bother.

Kleeba, by then well settled into her role at his parents' company, still asked after both - the farm and the company, in the same careful, practical order every time - the same kind of questions she'd once asked about his sleep and his meals, as though neither venture were quite finished being something she looked after. Saketh and Revati never once asked when he intended to slow down, only whether he was eating properly, which he understood, by then, to be their way of asking the same thing.

It was closer to the end of the fifth year - Vikram's toys now sold in three states, Devansh's hardware humming inside machines none of them would ever see, Sujay's monitors installed in a hospital wing two floors from where his grandmother had once waited forty unanswered minutes, Shaktiyuth's own two ventures both, finally, entirely unremarkable in their steadiness - that a business daily with considerably wider circulation than the old trade paper sent a reporter out, not for a quote about cattle or code, but for something closer to a profile: four college friends, one shared parcel of borrowed land, businesses that had, against every early prediction any of their own families had made, simply refused to fail.

He was not, by the end of that fifth year, a man who thought of himself as successful, though by any outside measure he plainly was. He thought of himself, most days, the way he'd thought of himself since the contest: as someone with a piece of work in front of him that wasn't finished yet, and would very likely never feel finished, no matter how many papers eventually got around to writing about it properly.

It didn't trouble him. It was, if anything, the part of the last five years he was most quietly glad of - that the wanting had never once run out.
