# Chapter Two: Who Is He

Sanya was waiting at the gate exactly where she always waited, one shoulder against the pillar, phone forgotten in her hand, and she took one look at Ananya coming up the road and said, "What happened to you."

Not a question. Sanya rarely asked questions when a flat statement would do the same work faster.

"Nothing happened," Ananya said, which even to her own ears sounded like the sort of thing a person said when something had very much happened.

"You're walking wrong."

"I'm walking exactly the way I always walk."

"You're walking like someone who didn't sleep and is pretending she did. I've known you since the third standard, Ananya. I know your walks." Sanya fell into step beside her anyway, already bored of the interrogation, already moving on to the actual matter of the morning. "Also, bad news - the bangle place pushed the order back a week, so tell your mother not to count on the good yellow set for the puja."

"I slept fine," Ananya said, a beat too late to be believed, and Sanya, who had already half-forgotten the question, glanced sideways at the timing of it and slowed down again.

"You didn't."

"I slept. I just - didn't sleep well, after."

"After what?" Sanya stopped walking altogether now, which she almost never did before the first lecture, and turned to look at her properly. Whatever Sanya saw in Ananya's face settled something in her own, some private calculation about how the rest of the morning was going to go. "Okay. What."

They found their usual bench under the gulmohar outside the block that never quite got the morning shade it needed, and Ananya sat with her bag still on her shoulder, working out - even now, even having rehearsed it half the walk here - that there was no version of this that would sound like anything other than what it was.

"I had a dream," she said.

Sanya waited, visibly deciding whether this required her full attention or could be handled while checking a message. She put the phone away. Full attention, then.

"There was a woman in it. Not - not a normal woman. She glowed. Not like a torch, like -" Ananya stopped, annoyed at herself, because she'd had the entire walk to find better words than this and had arrived with none. "It doesn't matter what she looked like. She told me to marry someone."

"Marry someone," Sanya repeated, carefully, the way you repeat a phrase back to make sure you heard it right and not because you plan to agree with it.

"His name is Shaktiyuth."

Sanya turned this over for a second, testing whether it belonged to anyone either of them actually knew. It didn't seem to. "Who is he?"

And this, Ananya understood as she opened her mouth to answer, was the part she could not have rehearsed on the walk over, because she hadn't fully known until this moment how much of it she was actually going to say out loud.

"I don't know him," she said. "I've never met him. I don't even know what he looks like." She said this plainly, without apologising for it, the way you'd state a fact that happened to be inconvenient rather than one you were ashamed of. "But I know things about him. I know he had a caretaker when he was a child - a woman named Kleeba, because his parents both worked and couldn't look after him themselves. And I know he was disabled. Not - not a small thing. Slow. He couldn't learn the way other children learned, couldn't play the games they played. He used to say her name wrong. Kleeba came out 'Kleem,' every single time, for years, and nobody ever managed to correct it out of him."

Sanya's face had gone very still, the particular stillness she wore when she was trying to be kind about something she found completely absurd.

"That's..." Sanya started, and didn't finish it, choosing instead: "That's very specific for a dream."

"I know how it sounds."

"Ananya. You don't know what he looks like. But you know his caretaker's name."

"I know," Ananya said again, because there was nothing else true to say to that.

"Where do his parents even live? Do you know that too?"

"No."

"Do you know his surname? His age? Whether he's - whether any of this is even happening right now, or happened years ago, or hasn't happened yet?"

"No," Ananya said, to all of it, and didn't look away while she said it, which was somehow the part that made Sanya stop asking. Not agreement. Just the recognition that Ananya had already asked herself every one of those questions, on the walk over, and had arrived at the gate without a single answer and without it changing anything at all.

She didn't tell Sanya about the rest - not properly, not in a way she could have made sound like anything but nonsense if she'd tried. There had been other things in the dream, later, softer, half-formed in a way the boy at the low table and the woman saying "Kleeba" a thousand times had never been - a racket held wrong in a hand that had learned to hold it right, a newspaper she couldn't read the words of, held up by her own hand though it wasn't her hand yet, a green place far away that meant something enormous and nothing at all. She didn't know what any of it meant. She didn't know, if she was honest with herself sitting here in the gulmohar's non-shade, whether it meant the boy from the clear part of the dream had grown into whatever those later pictures were, or whether he was, this morning, right now, still exactly as small and as slow as she'd seen him first. She only knew that both things had been shown to her, and that she believed the first with a certainty that frightened her a little, and held the second the way you hold something you aren't sure is yours to hold at all.

So she left it out. Not a lie. Just an editing - some things not for telling until you'd worked out yourself what they meant.

"So," Sanya said, in the tone of someone setting a difficult problem down carefully so it wouldn't spill. "A dream told you to marry a boy who might be disabled, whose name you know, whose face you don't, who you've never met, who you don't even know is still - however he was."

"When you say it like that -"

"How else is there to say it?"

Ananya didn't have an answer for that yet, either. She looked past Sanya at the gate, at the ordinary stream of the morning going on around them exactly as it always did - a scooter horn, someone's laugh two benches over, the gulmohar dropping one orange petal onto the path without any particular ceremony - and found, underneath the part of her that understood exactly how mad this sounded, a much steadier part that simply wasn't worried.

"I'm not asking you to believe it," she said finally. "I'm just telling you, because you're the only person I tell things to."

Sanya looked at her for a long moment - not the teasing look, not yet the worried one either, something new and searching in between the two, as if she were trying to work out which version of her best friend she was currently sitting next to. "Okay," she said, at last, and reached over to straighten the strap on Ananya's bag, an old, absent-minded gesture that meant more than anything she could have said. "Okay. Come on. We're going to be late."

They walked in together, and Sanya didn't bring it up again that day, and Ananya understood - correctly, though neither of them said so - that this was not because Sanya had let it go, but because she was still deciding what to say about it, and Sanya never said anything about anything until she'd decided properly.

It was only much later, changing out of the day's clothes that evening, that Ananya let herself think about the one part of the morning that had actually surprised her. Not Sanya's questions - she'd expected every one of them, had asked most of them of herself first. What surprised her was how little the questions had moved her. She had sat on that bench and watched her best friend of nine years look at her the way you'd look at someone who'd said something faintly worrying at a wedding, and she had not, for even a second, wanted to take any of it back.

She wondered, briefly, whether that should worry her more than it did. She decided, folding the clothes over the chair, that it probably should, and went to sleep anyway, entirely unworried, the name still sitting where it had sat all day - visible, settled, hers.
