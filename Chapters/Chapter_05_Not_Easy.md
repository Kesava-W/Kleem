# Chapter Five: Not Easy

It was ten days before Sanya brought it up again, which Ananya had come to understand, over nine years of friendship, was exactly how long Sanya needed to turn a thing over properly before she trusted herself to speak about it without saying something she'd regret.

Those ten days hadn't been silent, exactly - they'd eaten lunch together most afternoons, same as always, complained about the same professor, argued about the same film neither of them had actually watched yet - but there had been a carefulness underneath the ordinary noise of it, a subject both of them were stepping around without ever naming it directly. Ananya noticed. She said nothing, because she understood, better than Sanya perhaps realised, that some things needed exactly as long as they needed, and that hurrying Sanya toward an opinion had never once, in nine years, produced a better one.

They were on the library steps, waiting out the worst of the afternoon heat before the walk to the bus stand, when Sanya said, without any preamble at all, "I've been thinking about your dream."

"I know you have."

"You know?"

"You've been doing the face." Ananya didn't look up from her notes. "The one where you're arguing with me in your head and losing."

Sanya made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "I haven't been losing."

"You've been quiet for ten days about something you usually can't leave alone for ten minutes. That's losing."

Sanya was quiet again now, for a different reason, and when she finally spoke it was without any of the teasing edge she usually carried into difficult conversations, which told Ananya, before a single word landed, how seriously she'd taken this.

"I'm not going to tell you it's not real," Sanya said. "The dream. Whatever you saw, whoever you saw it from - I don't know what that was, and I'm not going to pretend I do. Maybe it means something. I genuinely don't know."

"But."

"But say it's true. Say every bit of it is exactly what it looked like." Sanya turned to face her properly now, the way she did when she wanted to be sure she wasn't going to be waved off halfway through. "You told me he was disabled. Badly enough that you knew his caretaker's name because he couldn't say his own words right. That's not a small thing to have seen, Ananya, even in a dream. And then you told me - you didn't say it plainly, but I heard it anyway - that there was other stuff. Later stuff. Things you couldn't explain, that felt like they meant something got better."

"There was."

"How sure are you that it did?"

Ananya didn't answer immediately, and Sanya let the silence sit there rather than filling it, which was its own kind of mercy.

"Because here's what worries me," Sanya went on, gentler now. "Not that you dreamed something. People dream all kinds of things. What worries me is that you've built an entire - I don't even know what to call it, a certainty, a whole shape of your future - around the *clear* part of that dream, which was the disability, and a *hazy* part you can't even describe to me properly, which is apparently the part where it all comes right. You're building your life on the half of the dream you're least sure about." She exhaled, the fight going out of her voice, replaced with something closer to plain worry. "Even if he did get better - even if some of it happened exactly the way you think - a childhood like that doesn't just disappear because it's over. It leaves things behind. Ways of being. You don't know this man, Ananya. You don't know if the boy from your dream grew into someone who healed from all of it, or someone who's still carrying every bit of it around, quietly, in ways a dream wouldn't show a stranger who's never met him."

It was, Ananya thought, listening to all of it without once looking away, the most careful and least cruel version of the warning anyone could have given her. Sanya wasn't mocking the dream. She wasn't calling her foolish. She was doing the harder thing - taking it entirely seriously, following it all the way through to its actual, practical consequences, and asking Ananya to look at what she'd find there.

"You're not wrong about any of that," Ananya said, finally.

"I know I'm not. That's what's frustrating me. You're not even going to argue."

"Would arguing change anything you just said?"

"No," Sanya admitted, and there was something almost funny in how reluctantly she said it, as if honesty had ambushed her mid-sentence. "That's the other frustrating part."

"What would I argue? You've described exactly what I know and exactly what I don't. I can't tell you the hazy part is true. I can't prove any of it to you, and I'm not going to pretend I can." She closed her notebook, unhurried, and looked out at the courtyard rather than at Sanya, not because she was avoiding the conversation but because what she had to say next needed saying plainly, without either of them performing it for the other. "I just know it. Not the way I know facts. The way I know which direction is north without checking, some mornings. I don't have a better word for it than that, and I know it's not an answer that would satisfy you if our places were reversed."

"It wouldn't," Sanya admitted.

"I'm not asking it to. I'm not asking you to carry any of this the way I'm carrying it. I just needed you to know I heard everything you said, and none of it changed anything, and that's not me being stubborn at you. It's just - " She searched for it, briefly, and let it go unfinished, because the honest ending to that sentence was simpler than any explanation she could build around it. "It's just true. The way you know things are true."

Sanya studied her for a long moment - not searching for a crack this time, Ananya understood, but something closer to memorising the shape of a decision she knew she wasn't going to be able to talk her out of. "Okay," she said, at last, the word carrying none of its usual lightness. "Okay. I said my piece. I'm not going to bring it up again unless something changes."

"Nothing's going to change."

"I know that too," Sanya said, and there was something almost sad in how readily she said it, as if she'd already begun grieving, a little, for whichever version of this turned out to be the wrong one. "Come on. We'll miss the bus."

She stood, shouldered her bag, and started down the steps without waiting to see whether Ananya would follow - not out of unkindness, but because some conversations were easier to end walking than sitting still, and Sanya had always understood, better than most people, exactly when a subject needed to be allowed to close itself rather than being closed by force. Ananya followed a few paces behind, watching the back of her friend's head, the particular set of her shoulders that meant she was still turning something over even now, and loved her, in that moment, more plainly than she had in a long while - not for agreeing with her, which she hadn't, but for taking the trouble to disagree properly, the way you only bother to disagree with someone whose life you actually care about the shape of.

---

Ananya watched her go, and sat a while longer on the library steps after Sanya had already gone ahead toward the bus stand, turning over the one part of the conversation she hadn't said aloud: that Sanya's warning hadn't frightened her half so much as it had clarified something she hadn't fully admitted to herself yet. She didn't know what she was waiting for. She didn't know if it would take a year or ten. She only knew, sitting there in the last of the afternoon heat, that whatever shape that waiting took, she intended to still be standing inside it when it finally arrived - not out of stubbornness, whatever Sanya wanted to call it, but because some certainties, once you had them properly, weren't things you could set back down again even if you tried.

Nothing did change, not for a long time.

The dream did not return. No new image arrived to fill in what the hazy half of it had left unclear. College went on being college - exams, deadlines, Sanya's own life filling up with its own crowded ordinary concerns - and somewhere underneath all of it, entirely without ceremony, Ananya's certainty simply became a fact of her life the way a scar becomes a fact of a body: always there, rarely thought about, never quite forgotten either. Neither of them brought Shaktiyuth's name up again. There was nothing new to say about him, and both of them, in their different ways, had already said everything they had.

It would be years before either of them had reason to think of him again.
