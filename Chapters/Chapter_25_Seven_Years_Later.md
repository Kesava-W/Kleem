# Chapter Twenty-Five: Seven Years Later

Seven years later.

The kitchen at that hour had the particular unhurried light of a house that had long since stopped needing to rush its mornings - sun coming in low and gold across the table, the kettle still ticking as it cooled, the whole ordinary machinery of a family getting itself out the door running exactly the way it ran most days, which was to say not quite smoothly, and not quite on time, and entirely without anyone particularly minding either.

"I don't *want* it," Vaishnavi announced, with the particular finality of someone who had clearly rehearsed the line in her head before deploying it, and pushed the bowl of poha half an inch further from her place at the table, as though the extra half-inch settled the matter beyond appeal.

"You wanted it yesterday," Ananya said, sliding it back with two fingers, the same unhurried patience she brought to every negotiation that had ever crossed her desk at Vikas Limited, though this particular client was considerably shorter and considerably less reasonable than any she'd managed there. "You asked Kleeba specially for extra peanuts in it yesterday."

"That was yesterday-me. Today-me doesn't like peanuts."

"Today-me is going to be very hungry by the time the bell rings at eleven."

Vaishnavi considered this with the grave seriousness of a girl weighing an argument she suspected, correctly, that she was losing, and ate one spoonful, mutinously, with the specific expression of a person doing someone else a great and unappreciated favour.

Across the kitchen, Shaktiyuth had her school bag open on the counter and was working through it with the same unhurried, methodical attention he gave everything - lunch box first, wedged so it wouldn't spill; the reading book she needed for Tuesdays, checked against a small laminated timetable stuck inside the flap that he'd made himself, some years back, once it became clear that a six-year-old's memory for which day needed which book could not be relied upon by anyone, including the six-year-old in question.

"You've put the wrong pencil box," Vaishnavi informed him, without looking up from her poha, in the tone of someone catching out a subordinate.

"I've put the pencil box you asked for on Sunday."

"I changed my mind on Monday."

"Then Monday-you should have told someone," Shaktiyuth said, entirely unbothered, and swapped it anyway, because he had learned, over six years of this exact kind of negotiation, that being right and being efficient were not always the same project.

Saketh watched this whole exchange from his chair by the window with the newspaper open and largely unread in his lap, the particular amused stillness of a grandfather who had long since stopped pretending he was going to intervene in any of it. Revati, at the stove, caught Ananya's eye over the top of Vaishnavi's head and smiled the smile of a woman who had watched this same small drama play out most mornings for two years running and never once tired of it.

Kleeba arrived, as she nearly always did on her way to the office, with a tin of something Revati hadn't asked for and would eat anyway, and set it on the counter with the particular brisk affection she still brought to the house, seven years and a company title later.

"You've lost weight," she told Shaktiyuth, by way of greeting, examining him with the frank, unembarrassed scrutiny of someone who had once measured his whole existence in whether he'd finished his milk.

"I haven't."

"You have. Sit. Eat something before you start telling me I haven't."

He sat. He ate something, without argument, in a manner Ananya had genuinely never once seen him extend to a board meeting, an investor, or - on one memorable occasion two years ago - his own father. There was a particular looseness that came into him only around Kleeba, a total absence of whatever careful, measured stillness he wore for the rest of the world, and Ananya had long ago stopped being surprised by it and started simply enjoying the sight of it instead, the way you enjoy a rare weather.

"Kleem," he said, once he'd finished, using the old name with the specific fondness of a joke thirty years old and never once worn out by the repeating, and Kleeba swatted his shoulder with the folded end of her dupatta, delighted despite herself, the way she always was.

"Don't call me that in front of the child."

"She's heard it before."

"Papa says it wrong on purpose," Vaishnavi informed the room, with the weary authority of someone explaining an obvious fact to slower people. "I've heard him say it properly. He only says it wrong for Kleeba-aunty."

Nobody corrected her. It was, Ananya thought, watching all of it from across the table, entirely and exactly true.

"Straighten that before you go," Kleeba added, nodding at the framed photograph of the four founders on their contest-winning day, which had hung very slightly crooked on the kitchen wall for as long as Ananya had lived in this house and which nobody but Kleeba ever seemed to notice enough to mention. Shaktiyuth glanced at it, and something crossed his face too quick to properly name - amusement, mostly, and something else underneath it, older and quieter - before he reached over on his way out and set it right without a word.

---

They left for school with time to spare, for once, Vaishnavi installed in the back seat with her bag and her still-half-eaten poha grievance largely forgotten, Ananya driving with the window cracked to let the morning in. The city outside had changed in seven years in ways too gradual to notice day by day and too obvious to miss all at once - a flyover where there hadn't been one, a mall where a field used to run, the same slow, unhurried reshaping that happened to every place given enough years to happen in.

Her phone rang through the car's speaker somewhere past the second signal.

"Papa," Ananya said, and then, before he could get a word out, "we're driving, you're on speaker, mind what you say."

"I always mind what I say," Suraj said, with the particular dryness that had, over seven years, become almost indistinguishable from warmth in him. "Where's my granddaughter."

"Right here," Vaishnavi said, leaning forward against her seatbelt as far as it would allow. "Nana, I didn't want to eat my poha and Mama made me anyway."

"Very wise of your mother," Suraj said. "I once didn't want to eat my dinner and your great-grandfather made me sit at the table until midnight. You got off easily."

"Midnight?"

"Ask your Nani. She'll tell you it was closer to one."

Sandhya's voice came in over his, laughing, correcting some detail of the story Ananya only half-caught, and for a few minutes the car filled with the easy, overlapping noise of two grandparents competing gently for a six-year-old's attention, and Ananya drove through it smiling, glancing once at Shaktiyuth in the passenger seat, who was watching the exchange with the same quiet, private satisfaction he brought to very few other things.

They said their goodbyes at the school gate the way they said them most mornings now - unremarkable, easy, nothing left in any of it that still ached the way it once must have, though Ananya knew, without either of her parents ever quite saying so, exactly how much distance they'd all had to travel to arrive at *unremarkable*.

---

Vaishnavi went in through the gate without a backward glance, the particular brisk independence of a child who had stopped needing to watch her parents' car pull away to feel certain they'd be there again at three. Ananya watched her go anyway, the way she always did, until the small blue uniform disappeared properly into the crowd of other small uniforms just like it.

"She'll be fine," Shaktiyuth said, not because Ananya had said otherwise, but because he'd learned, over six years of exactly this moment, precisely when she needed to hear it said aloud.

"I know." She pulled back out into the traffic, toward the farm on one side of the city and the software office on the other, toward whichever one of them today's schedule called for first. "I just like watching her walk in like she owns the place."

"She does rather think she does."

"Wonder where she gets that."

He didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer, and she let it sit between them the comfortable way you let a joke sit once it's already landed.

She thought, driving, of a dream from a very long time ago - a woman made of light, a name arriving whole before she'd ever earned the right to it, a boy at a low table with his brow furrowed over nothing at all. She had known, that night, with a certainty she'd never once been able to explain to anyone who asked her to, including herself. She had not known, then, that certainty would have to survive six years of nothing, a dream half-believed even by the people who loved her, a wedding arranged to someone else entirely, a night that had asked more of everyone in it than any of them should reasonably have had to give. She had known only the one thing, small and sturdy and entirely sufficient in the end: that it was true, and that she would spend a long time finding out what it cost to be right about it.

She knew now what it had cost, in full, because Shaktiyuth had finally told her - not that first year, when the telling would only have added her grief to his, but two years into their marriage, one ordinary evening when Vaishnavi was still a name they were only just beginning to try out loud for someone who didn't exist yet. He'd told her about the wall, and the pillar, and the story her father had built out of his own blood to send Ankush's men into the forest instead of after them any other way. He'd told her, too, what it had actually cost - that Vikas Limited, tangled for two years in the mess of who inherited what from a dead man with a dead heir, had finally been sold off in pieces the year after the wedding, her father keeping nothing of it but the apartment and, as he put it to her afterward with more peace than she'd expected to find in the words, "considerably more time than I've had in thirty years to enjoy the family I nearly lost getting it." Suraj himself had never once said any of this to her directly, not before and not since; it was Shaktiyuth's to tell, because he was the one who'd known it all along, and he'd waited, he told her, only until he judged she was finally ready to carry it rather than simply grieve it.

She had cried, that evening, for the first time in years, not from surprise but from the particular relief of a suspicion finally allowed to become a fact. She thought of her father some mornings still, the particular stillness he'd carried for months after that night, slowly softening year by year into something that finally looked, most days, like an ordinary man simply glad of how things had turned out - which she now understood, rather than merely sensed, he entirely was. She had known, even at nineteen, that all of it would cost something. She had simply never once doubted, not for a single one of the years in between, that it would be worth exactly what it asked of her.

"What are you thinking about," Shaktiyuth asked, glancing over.

"Nothing," Ananya said, and smiled, and meant, as she so often did with him, something closer to *everything, and I'll tell you properly another time* - and drove on, toward the rest of an ordinary, unremarkable, entirely hard-won day.
