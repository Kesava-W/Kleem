# Chapter Fifteen: What Suraj Knows

Sandhya waited until she heard Ananya's door close upstairs before she said anything at all, which was, Suraj had learned over twenty-six years of marriage, always the more dangerous kind of waiting - not the silence of a woman with nothing to say, but the silence of one carefully building the order she meant to say it in.

"Explain it to me," she said, once the floor above had gone quiet. "You've never once fully trusted that man, not since he bought his way into the company. I've watched you go stiff every time his name comes up at a board dinner. And now you want to bind our daughter to his family for the rest of her life?"

"I don't want it."

"Then why are you arranging it?"

Suraj turned his own cup a slow half-circle on the table, the same unconscious habit he'd had since long before she'd married him, buying himself a moment he didn't entirely know how to spend. "Because refusing him outright costs us something I'm not ready to pay yet. He holds enough of Vikas Limited now that an open insult to his family isn't a small thing, Sandhya. It's the kind of thing a man like Niraj remembers, and acts on, in his own time, in ways I can't always predict in advance."

"So you'd trade Ananya's whole life to avoid inconveniencing a shareholder."

"I'd stall him," Suraj said, sharper than he meant to, and softened it immediately, because the sharpness wasn't for her. "I don't believe this marriage happens, Sandhya. Not really. I think there's a version of the next several months where I manage this carefully enough that it quietly falls apart on its own, without me ever having to be the one who refused him to his face."

"You're gambling our daughter's next six months on a feeling."

"I'm gambling considerably more than her next six months on it, and I know that better than you're giving me credit for." He hadn't meant for that much to come through in his voice, and regretted it slightly once it had - not because it wasn't true, but because it gave away more of the shape of his own worry than he'd intended to share tonight. "I've watched this man work for two years now, Sandhya. Quietly. Patiently. Nothing he does looks like what it actually is until considerably later than it should. I don't fully trust my own read on how far ahead of us he's already planning."

"That's not a plan. That's a hope wearing a plan's clothes."

It was, he privately conceded, not an unfair description. "It's what I have," he said instead, which was closer to honest than he generally allowed himself to be with her about the company at all.

Sandhya studied him for a long moment, the particular searching look she gave him when she suspected, correctly, that he was withholding the better half of whatever he actually knew. "And the boy? Ananya's boy. You looked at her like she'd struck you, when she said his name."

Suraj hadn't realised it had shown quite that plainly. He considered, briefly, denying it, and decided against the effort - Sandhya had never once in twenty-six years failed to see through that particular evasion, and there seemed little point starting tonight.

There was something else, too, sitting underneath the recognition, that he wasn't yet ready to put into words for her or for himself - a half-formed unease he couldn't fully trace to its source. He'd heard the name once before the newspaper piece, months earlier, in passing, from Niraj himself, at a function neither of them had particularly wanted to attend. Someone had mentioned a young entrepreneur's rapid rise, admiringly, the way people mentioned such things at those functions, and Niraj's face had done something brief and strange at the name - gone, before Suraj could properly read it, replaced immediately by the same easy warmth Niraj wore for everyone. Suraj had thought nothing of it at the time. He thought rather more of it now, turning the memory over in the quiet of his own dining room, and still couldn't have said what exactly it meant.

"I know the name," he admitted. "Not the man. I've never met him. But Vikas Limited does business, however distantly, in circles that overlap his, and a man who takes two failing ideas and turns them into what his have become in five years doesn't stay unnoticed for long, not in the world I work in. There was a piece in one of the business dailies not long ago. Four college friends, one field, a boy who was supposedly disabled as a child and built two companies before thirty regardless." He shook his head slightly, still turning the fact over as though it might yield something further on a second pass. "I remember thinking, reading it, that I hoped I never found myself on the wrong side of a man who'd already survived that much before he'd even started building anything."

"You think our daughter has found herself the one man in this city difficult enough to actually stand up to Niraj."

"I think," Suraj said, carefully, "that if even half of what I've heard about him is true, he isn't someone who gets pushed anywhere he hasn't already decided to go. Which should frighten me, given what I'm currently trying to arrange around him without his knowledge or his consent." He allowed himself, for the first time that evening, something close to a rueful smile. "It doesn't, entirely. I'm not sure what that says about me."

Sandhya didn't return the smile. Her face had settled into the particular stillness she wore when fear had finally caught up with confusion and found somewhere permanent to sit. "You said, back there, that you know he isn't someone who can be pushed over. Do you hear how that sounds, Suraj? You're describing our future son-in-law like a rival, not a match."

"Maybe that's exactly what he is. A rival, or something close enough to one that the distinction won't matter much longer." He said it half to himself, working through the shape of a thought he hadn't fully finished having. "I'm not sure Niraj understands what he's actually invited into this family, arranging a match against a man like that. I'm not sure I understand it either, not yet. But I'd rather find out slowly, watching from a careful distance, than force the question before I know which way it's likely to fall."

"What do we do, Suraj? If any part of what she said tonight is real - the dream, the Goddess, all of it - what exactly are you and I supposed to do about a daughter who's already decided her whole life around something neither of us can see or touch or argue with?"

Suraj didn't have an answer for her. He turned the cup another slow half-circle instead, buying himself a silence he had no honest way to fill, and understood, watching his wife wait for a reassurance he wasn't yet able to give her, that the coming months were going to ask considerably more of both of them than either was currently prepared to offer.

"I don't know yet," he said, finally, which was the truest thing he'd said to her all evening. "I'm going to need some time to work out what we do."

It would be some weeks yet - though neither of them could have known it, sitting there with the plates still uncleared and the house gone quiet around them - before he worked out anything at all resembling an answer, and considerably longer before he judged himself ready to act on it without telling her what it was going to cost.
