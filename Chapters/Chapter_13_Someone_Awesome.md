# Chapter Thirteen: Someone Awesome

They were at Priya's wedding shopping, of all places, trailing behind a bride-to-be who couldn't decide between two nearly identical shades of gold and had roped both of them in as tie-breakers neither felt remotely qualified to be. The shop was the usual chaos of a Saturday before a wedding season - three other parties crowded around three other counters, a radio somewhere behind the register playing something too cheerful for the volume it was set at, an uncle two counters down loudly relitigating a price he'd already agreed to twice. Sanya had appointed herself chief bangle inspector within the first five minutes, holding each option up to the light with the seriousness of a jeweller rather than a bystander, and it was in the middle of this performance that she set one down and said, apropos of absolutely nothing, "You know you're going to end up more married than any of us, don't you."

"I'm not sure that's a real category."

"It's real. Priya's marrying a boy from her cousin's office whose biggest personality trait is that he owns a nice car. You're marrying someone who came back from being severely disabled to running two companies before thirty, after a Goddess personally arranged the whole thing." Sanya held up the bangle again, considering it against the light, entirely too pleased with the comparison she'd just made. "I'm just saying. When the stories get told later, yours is going to be the one people actually want to hear twice."

"You make it sound like a competition."

"Everything's a competition, Ananya, we just don't all admit it out loud." Sanya grinned, the old easy grin, six years and one apology past the version of this conversation that used to end in worry rather than teasing. "I'm serious, though. Do you understand what you've actually got waiting for you? Some women spend their whole lives hoping for someone decent. You're getting someone *awesome*, and you didn't even have to date badly first to figure out who he was."

Ananya felt the heat climb up her own face before she could stop it, ducking her head over a tray of bangles she wasn't actually looking at. "Stop it."

"I'm not going to stop it. You're blushing. This is the best part of my week." Sanya bumped her shoulder, affectionate, unrelenting. "When do the rest of us get to meet this awesome man of yours, by the way?"

"When I meet him."

"You haven't - " Sanya paused, some of the teasing dropping out of her voice as an actual thought caught up with the joke. "Wait. You genuinely haven't tried to reach him? He runs two companies, Ananya, he's not exactly hiding. You could write to him tomorrow if you wanted to."

"I know I could."

"So why haven't you?"

It was a fair question, and one Ananya had turned over often enough in the weeks since the newspaper that she had an answer ready, even if it wasn't one she'd fully explained to anyone yet, not even Sanya. "Because I don't think it's mine to start," she said, slowly, choosing the words with more care than the wedding-shopping noise around them really warranted. "The dream came to me. Whatever happens next, I think it has to come to him too, in its own time, the way mine did. I'm not going to write him a letter explaining that a Goddess told me to marry him six years before I saw his photograph. Can you imagine what that would sound like, arriving out of nowhere, to a man who doesn't know I exist?"

"When you put it that way, no. That would sound completely unhinged."

"Thank you."

"I still think you're allowed to want it to move faster."

"I do want that," Ananya admitted, quiet enough that it nearly got lost under Priya calling both their names from three stalls down, demanding a verdict on the gold. "I want it more than I let myself think about, most days. I just don't know how to make it happen without breaking whatever it is that's been working, all this time, by not being forced."

---

They gave Priya their verdict when she finally called them over - the lighter gold, unanimously, though neither of them had actually been paying close enough attention to have a real opinion. Priya, satisfied, held the chosen bangle up against her wrist one last time and said, entirely without malice, "Sanya's right, you know. Your story's going to make ours all look very boring in ten years." She said it lightly, the way you say something you don't expect to be taken seriously, and moved on to the next decision before either of them could answer - which was, Ananya thought, its own small mercy, since she wasn't sure what answer she'd have given if Priya had actually waited for one.

They settled onto a bench outside the shop afterward, waiting for the auto Sanya had called.

"Can I ask you something else," Sanya said, "and you don't have to answer if it's too much."

"Go on."

"Have you told your parents? Any of it. The dream, the name, the newspaper - any of it at all?"

Ananya was quiet for long enough that the answer arrived before she said it.

"No," she admitted, finally. "I haven't told them anything."

"Ananya."

"I know." She looked down at her hands, turning her own bare wrist the way Priya had turned the bangles inside, as if she might find some answer written there that she hadn't thought to look for yet. "I keep meaning to. And then I imagine actually saying it out loud to my father - *a Goddess came to me in a dream six years ago and told me to marry a man I've still never met* - and I lose my nerve every single time. It sounded mad enough telling you. You're my best friend. He's going to want proof, and reasons, and a plan, and I don't have any of those things to give him yet. I only have the truth, and I don't think the truth, on its own, is going to be enough for him."

Sanya didn't have an answer to that, not immediately, and for once she didn't try to manufacture one just to fill the silence. She simply put her hand over Ananya's on the bench, the small solid weight of it saying most of what needed saying.

"For what it's worth," Sanya said, after a while, "I think you're wrong about one part of it."

"Which part."

"That the truth won't be enough for him. Your father's known you your whole life, Ananya. He knows you don't go mad over nothing, and he knows you've never once needed proof to be right about a person's character before you'd actually met them - remember what you said about my cousin's fiancé, three years before anyone else caught on to him?" Sanya shook her head, half amused at her own memory. "I'm not saying it'll be easy. I'm saying you might be underestimating him."

"Maybe," Ananya allowed. She sat with it a moment, watching the uncle two counters down finally lose his price argument, watching Priya reappear in the shop doorway laughing at something the shopkeeper had said. "Not today, though. I'm not doing it standing on a pavement after bangle shopping, with my nerve half spent on you already."

"Fair. When, then?"

Ananya turned the question over the way she'd turned the bracelet, and found, somewhat to her own surprise, that an actual answer was sitting there waiting to be picked up rather than avoided. "This week," she said. "Properly, at dinner, sitting down, the way it deserves to be said instead of blurted at him between courses because I've lost my nerve again. I'll pick an evening he's not distracted by the office, and I'll say it plainly, start to finish, and I won't let myself soften it into something smaller just to make it easier to hear."

"You're actually going to do it."

"I'm actually going to do it." She said it again, quieter, testing the weight of it now that it was out of her mouth and no longer just a thought she could keep circling. "I've been telling myself *not yet* for six weeks. I don't think *not yet* is a plan. It's just a nicer word for never getting round to it."

Sanya studied her for a moment, something unusually serious under the teasing she'd worn all afternoon. "For what it's worth, I think he's going to take it better than you're bracing for."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then he doesn't, and you'll have said it anyway, which is more than *not yet* was ever going to get you." Sanya squeezed her hand once and let go as the auto pulled up at the kerb. "Text me after. Whatever happens."

"I will."

"Not today," Sanya said, standing, offering her hand up to pull Ananya to her feet, "but soon. I'm holding you to it."

"Soon," Ananya agreed, and meant it, in the particular way she'd meant very few things since a library bench six years before this one.
