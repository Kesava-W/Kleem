# Chapter Twenty: Room of Anu

Six weeks was, in the end, exactly enough time to plan a wedding neither of the two people whose names were on the invitation had ever agreed to want.

Ananya had spent them the way she imagined a person spent a slow-moving illness - going through the motions the day required of her, fittings and guest lists and a hundred small decisions about flowers she couldn't have named a preference for if her life had depended on it, while some other, steadier part of her simply waited, the way she'd been waiting for six years already, for a fact she'd never once stopped believing to finally catch up with the calendar. Sandhya had grown, over those same six weeks, quietly and carefully happy in a way that cost Ananya more to watch than any argument would have - fabric samples held up to the light, a caterer's menu read aloud twice over breakfast, a mother building a wedding for a daughter who had never once told her she wanted it, because it was easier, Ananya supposed, than sitting with the alternative.

She hadn't seen Shaktiyuth again since the gate. She hadn't heard from him, hadn't found any way to reach him that didn't risk the small, careful distance her father seemed to be quietly enforcing around her these six weeks - fewer errands out of the house, a driver who always somehow had somewhere else he needed to be first, an evening walk she'd once taken alone now gently, wordlessly accompanied. She noticed it. She said nothing about it. She had learned, watching Suraj for twenty-five years, that a thing he wasn't ready to explain was rarely a thing worth demanding an explanation for before he chose to give one.

The wedding was tomorrow.

She lay awake that last night with the lights off and the fan turning slowly overhead, past midnight, past the hour when even the city outside her window finally quieted into something like sleep, and did not cry, because she had done all her crying weeks ago and found, somewhat to her own surprise, that it hadn't changed anything either way. She thought instead about a garden she'd never seen, and a caretaker whose name she knew without ever having met her, and the particular, absurd certainty that had carried her this far without once needing anyone else's permission to believe it. She held onto that certainty now the way you hold onto a rail in the dark - not because you can see where it leads, only because you know, with your whole body, that letting go of it would be worse.

The knock, when it came, was so quiet she almost took it for the fan.

She sat up. It came again - two knuckles, once, twice, deliberately soft - and something in her chest went very still and very awake at the same time, the way it had on the third floor at Vikas Limited six weeks and a lifetime ago. She crossed the room barefoot and opened the door.

Her father stood in the corridor. Beside him, half in shadow from the passage light he hadn't turned fully on, stood Shaktiyuth.

Neither man said anything. Suraj looked at her for one long moment - something in his face she didn't have time to read properly, gone as fast as it had arrived - and then simply stepped back, out of the doorway, making room the way you make room for a thing you've already decided you're not going to stop.

She didn't ask how. She didn't ask why her father, of all people, was the one standing there making this possible instead of the one raising the house against it. Later there would be time for all of it - how he'd got past the gate, how her father had found him first, what exactly had passed between two men who had never once met before tonight in the sixty seconds it must have taken to cross the compound. Right now there was only the fact of him, standing in her doorway at half past midnight in clothes still damp from wherever he'd come from, and she closed the distance between them before either of them could think of a single thing worth saying first.

The hug was not gentle. She didn't mean it to be. She held onto him the way you hold onto something you've spent six years being told, gently and less gently, that you were foolish to believe in, and he held her back just as hard, his chin against the top of her head, both of them breathing too fast for either to manage speech for a long moment that neither seemed inclined to shorten.

"You came," she said finally, into his shoulder, which was not a sensible thing to say to a man standing directly in front of her, and she said it anyway.

"I told you I would," he said, quietly, close to her ear. "You only had to wait long enough for me to find the door."

She pulled back far enough to look at him properly. He looked, she thought, exactly as he had at the gate six weeks ago and nothing at all like it - the same stillness, the same unhurried attention, but underneath it now something more urgent than either, a man who had spent an unknown number of minutes crossing an unknown number of obstacles to reach this one doorway and had not yet allowed himself to feel any of it.

"How did you even -"

"Later," he said, not unkindly, glancing past her at the dark room behind. "Ask me everything later. I intend to still be here to answer it."

Suraj's voice, from the doorway, cut through it - low, urgent, none of the careful stillness he'd worn all evening left in it now. "There isn't time for this."

They broke apart. Ananya looked at her father properly for the first time since he'd stepped back to let them through, and found him already turned half toward the corridor, listening for something neither of them could hear yet, his whole body pitched forward like a man counting down a number only he could see.

"Papa -"

He didn't answer at once. For one strange, suspended moment he simply looked at her, something moving behind his face too fast and too complete for her to read any single part of it, and Ananya understood, without knowing why, that whatever this night was costing him, he had already weighed it and found it worth paying - that the hesitation wasn't him reconsidering, only him allowing himself, for one held breath, to feel the weight of a thing already decided before he set it down and moved on.

"Go." He said it flatly, not unkindly, the single word doing the work of everything he clearly didn't have the minutes left to explain. "Now, Ananya. Whatever you're going to take with you, take it in one hand and go. The car's at the side gate, not the front. The guard's chair is empty there tonight, and it will stay that way long enough for you to be gone."

She didn't ask him how he knew that either. She grabbed the one bag she'd packed and hidden three days ago against a hope she hadn't fully admitted to herself she was carrying, and followed Shaktiyuth into the corridor, her father falling into step half a pace behind them both, the whole apartment dark and silent around the three of them - her mother's door closed at the far end of the hall, no light beneath it, no sound from within it. Ananya slowed, just slightly, glancing that way.

"She's asleep," Suraj said, before she could ask. "Let her stay that way. It's kinder, tonight, than the alternative."

She didn't argue. There would be time, later, to decide whether he'd been right about that. There wasn't time now for anything except the stairs, taken quickly and quietly, and the side gate standing open exactly where he'd said it would be, the little wooden stool the night guard usually sat on pushed back against the wall and empty, and the ordinary, unremarkable car waiting just beyond it in the dark.

At the gate, she turned back once. Her father stood a few steps behind them, in the doorway's spill of light, not moving to follow any further, and something in the way he stood there - very straight, very still, watching her the way a man watches something he isn't certain he'll get to see again - snagged at her enough that she stopped, one hand still on the gate.

"Papa. Come with us. At least to the car."

"No." He said it gently, but there was no give in it at all. "This part isn't mine to finish. Go, beta. Both of you. Please."

She didn't understand it, not then, not for a long time afterward - the particular weight in *please*, a word she had almost never heard her father use in her whole life, the careful, contained thing in his face that looked less like fear for her than fear of what he had already set in motion and could no longer call back. She would think of it again and again in the years that followed, turning it over the way you turn over a sentence someone said to you once in passing, that only later turns out to have meant everything.

Shaktiyuth took her hand. She went.

The last thing she saw, looking back through the rear window as the car pulled away into the empty street, was her father standing exactly where they'd left him, at the open gate, not waving, not calling out - simply watching, very straight, very still, in a way she had no name for yet and would spend a long time afterward trying to find one for.
