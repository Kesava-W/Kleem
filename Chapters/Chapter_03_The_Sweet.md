# Chapter Three: The Sweet

The house had been Saketh's mother's before it was theirs, and Revati had kept the garden almost exactly as she'd found it - the guava tree too close to the boundary wall, the bed of marigolds that came up orange and stubborn every year whether anyone watered them or not - because some inheritances, she'd decided early on, were better left alone than improved. It was a good house for two people who worked as hard as they did to come home to. Quiet in the particular way a house is quiet when the people in it are tired in a satisfied sort of way, not an empty one.

They had built the company themselves, the two of them, over eleven years - nothing large, nothing that made the papers, but theirs, entirely, in a way that mattered more to both of them than size ever could have. Saketh handled the clients and the numbers. Revati handled everything Saketh described, not unkindly, as "the part that actually keeps people working for us," which was mostly true.

"You should slow down," Saketh told her, most evenings, with the particular lack of conviction of a man saying a thing he knows will change nothing.

"I will," Revati always said. "After."

"After what?"

"After whatever's next." It was, between the two of them, an old and comfortable joke - there was always a next, and Revati had never once, in eleven years, chosen to slow down ahead of it.

Between the two of them they had built something solid enough that when Revati told him, seven months along and glowing with it in the specific way her mother had always sworn brought good luck to a house, that she intended to keep working right up until she couldn't, Saketh had not argued. He knew better than to argue with a decision Revati had already finished making.

That autumn, the company closed its best year yet - a contract Saketh had chased for the better part of eight months, finally signed, finally real. The house filled up, over the following fortnight, with the ordinary evidence of good news travelling through people who knew them: boxes of mithai arriving with drivers and cousins and the odd business associate too polite to come empty-handed, more sweets than two people and one household help could reasonably eat, stacked on the sideboard in the front room until Revati started sending the extra boxes on to the neighbours rather than let them go to waste. One box came from an old friend of Saketh's from their early years in business, sent round by driver rather than brought by hand, as his gifts always seemed to arrive these days - generous, prompt, and never once accompanied by the man himself, a small habit of his that Saketh had noticed only enough to think, briefly and without much weight, that he ought to have him over properly one of these months.

She didn't remember, later, which box the piece she ate had come from. That detail simply hadn't seemed worth keeping, at the time. Why would it have.

---

The fever came on a Tuesday, mild enough at first that Revati assumed it was nothing - the ordinary exhaustion of a body doing extra work, she told Saketh, waving off his concern along with the thermometer he kept trying to press on her. By Thursday it wasn't nothing. By Saturday, Saketh had stopped going to the office at all, and the family doctor had been joined by a second opinion, and then a third, none of whom could agree on very much beyond the fact that whatever this was, it did not match anything they expected to see in a healthy woman seven months into an easy pregnancy.

It lasted eleven days. Revati remembered almost none of them clearly - a blur of ceiling fan and Saketh's hand and a nurse's voice reading out numbers that meant nothing to her - and then, on the twelfth day, it simply lifted, the way fevers sometimes do, with no more explanation for its leaving than it had given for its arrival. The doctors called it viral. One of them, more honest than the rest, admitted he was calling it viral because he had no better word and didn't like leaving a blank on the chart.

Nobody suggested it might have been anything else. There was nothing, in that room, in that week, pointing toward any hand in it at all - only a woman who had been unwell and then was not, and a husband who stopped being able to sleep properly for a few weeks after, for reasons he couldn't have explained even to himself. Revati herself let the question go long before Saketh did, on the whole, though it never entirely left her - a small, unfinished sum she'd find herself doing again at odd hours for years afterward, lying awake some nights turning over exactly which of that fortnight's dozen kindnesses might have carried, without anyone meaning it to, something other than good wishes.

Revati went back to work eleven days after that. The pregnancy, everyone agreed, seemed to have come through it fine.

---

Shaktiyuth was born in the spring, small and loud and, for the first year of his life, entirely ordinary in every way a new parent hopes for. It was only afterward - slowly, the way these things are always slow, a delayed word here, a missed milestone there, nothing any single doctor would commit to until enough of them had piled up to make a pattern - that Revati began keeping a private list in her head of things her son was not yet doing that the books said he should be.

The diagnosis, when it finally arrived, arrived without drama. A specialist in a quiet office, kind in the practiced way of someone who has delivered this news many times before, telling them plainly that their son's development was significantly behind what was typical, that they could not yet say how far behind he would remain, and that they should prepare themselves for the possibility - not the certainty, he was careful to say, only the possibility - that this might not change.

Saketh asked exactly one question. "What does he need from us."

The specialist, visibly unused to that being the first question a parent asked - most parents, in his experience, asked *why*, or *how long*, or *are you certain* - told him: patience, consistency, more hands than two working parents could reliably provide on their own. He added, carefully, that some children in Shaktiyuth's position responded to early intervention, and some did not, and that there was no honest way yet to say which kind of child theirs would turn out to be.

"Then we'll assume the difficult version," Saketh said, "and be glad if we're wrong."

It was, Revati thought, watching her husband fold the specialist's printed notes into his jacket pocket with the same unhurried care he gave a signed contract, exactly the right way to hear it.

Revati didn't cry in the office. She cried once, that night, alone in the bathroom with the tap running so Saketh wouldn't hear, and never again after that - not because the grief passed, but because she decided, somewhere in that one closed-door hour, that grief was a thing she would allow herself exactly once and then set down, because a child who needed patience and consistency did not need a mother who spent her mornings crying over what he wasn't.

"We'll manage it," she told Saketh, over breakfast the next day, in the tone she used for business problems that had solutions, even difficult ones. "We just need to work out how."

---

How, it turned out, meant someone in the house full-time - someone who wasn't running a company by day, someone patient enough for a child who took four times as long to learn anything as other children did and didn't seem to mind the slowness half as much as the adults around him minded it for him.

They interviewed six women before Kleeba. Revati remembered, afterward, very little about the other five - competent enough, all of them, references in order, nothing wrong with any of it and nothing that made Revati look at Saketh across the room the way she did on the sixth interview, a small private signal they'd developed over eleven years of running a business together: *this one.*

Kleeba was plain-spoken, unhurried, not given to the soft, performative sympathy some of the others had offered when Revati explained the diagnosis - the tilted head, the hand pressed briefly to the chest, the *ah, poor child* that always arrived before they'd even asked a single question about him. Kleeba asked no such thing. She asked what he ate, and whether he slept through the night, and what he did with his hands when he was frightened, three questions none of the other five had thought to ask, and only once she had answers to all three did she say, simply, "Children learn. Some take the long road. I don't mind a long road."

Nothing about it was dramatic. It was, Revati thought later, exactly the right amount of ordinary for what they needed - a woman who had clearly cared for a difficult child before and had not found the difficulty worth mentioning as though it were a hardship done to her.

She started the following Monday. Revati showed her the nursery, the kitchen, the particular way Shaktiyuth liked his milk warmed and no warmer, and left for the office an hour late that first morning, standing at the gate longer than she needed to, watching through the front window as Kleeba settled cross-legged on the floor beside her son with the unhurried patience of a woman who had already decided this was going to take exactly as long as it took, and who did not, unlike so many of the others, seem to mind.

Revati got into the car. She did not look back a second time. There would be, she told herself, driving to work with the ordinary weight of an ordinary Monday settling back over her, years of mornings like this one now - and there was no sense spending this first one anything other than the way she meant to spend all the rest: getting on with it.
