# Chapter Sixteen: The Garden

He couldn't sleep, that night, for no reason he could have named - not the useful kind of wakefulness that came with a problem worth solving, just the ordinary restlessness of a body that had decided, on its own schedule, that the night wasn't finished with him yet. He went out to the garden a little after midnight, the way he sometimes did, barefoot on grass still faintly warm from the day, and found Her standing beneath the guava tree as though She had always been standing there and he had simply never happened to look.

He did not startle. He understood, later, that this should perhaps have alarmed him more than it did - a figure made of quiet light, standing motionless in his own garden at midnight - but in the moment itself there was nothing in him that read Her as anything other than exactly, entirely right, the way a room feels right once the one missing piece of furniture finally arrives in it.

He crossed the grass toward Her without hurrying. She did not move to meet him halfway. She simply waited, the way She had waited, he understood without being told, for a very long time before this night - patient in a manner that had nothing to do with time the way he measured it.

The house behind him was dark and quiet, his parents long asleep, the whole ordinary architecture of his life carrying on exactly as it always did on the other side of a wall he had just walked past without looking back at it once. It occurred to him, distantly, that most people would have called out - woken someone, fetched a phone, done something to mark or verify or witness what was happening to them. He did none of it. Whatever was standing under the guava tree had not come for an audience.

*Ananya,* the thought arrived, whole and complete, the way his own name had once arrived to a boy who could only say half of it. Not spoken. Simply known, the instant he was close enough to know it. A woman's face he had never seen, a name he had never heard, and beneath both, a certainty so total it required no further evidence than its own arrival. *Marry her.*

He stood with that a moment, turning it over the unhurried way he turned over everything that mattered, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that no part of him wanted to argue with it. He had spent twenty-seven years on this particular kind of scrutiny - eleven of them lost to a slowness nobody had expected him to outgrow, the rest spent quietly, methodically becoming a man capable of questioning anything put in front of him - and this was, he noted with a kind of wry private clarity, the one thing in all that time he found himself entirely unwilling to interrogate.

"I understand," he said, and his own voice sounded strange to him in the garden's stillness, the first spoken words either of them had offered the other.

She smiled - not with triumph, not with relief, something quieter than either, the smile of someone watching a thing arrive precisely on the schedule she had always privately known it would keep.

He looked at Her for a long moment then, really looked, the way he looked at a system when he was trying to see past its surface to the shape underneath, and understood, with the same quiet totality the name Ananya had arrived with, exactly who stood before him. Not merely a goddess. *The* Goddess - Durga, in the aspect his grandmother had once tried, without much success, to explain to a boy too young and too slow to follow her - Paradevata, the one behind every lesser name given to Her, the one his own childhood mouth had been calling, wrongly and endlessly and without ever once knowing it, for the whole of nine unrepeatable years.

"Durga," he said, quietly, testing the word the way he'd once tested a stranger's name, and felt something settle into place at the saying of it that even the silent certainty a moment before had not quite managed to settle. "Paradevata."

She did not confirm it. She did not need to. Her smile only deepened, very slightly, the private, satisfied smile of someone hearing herself correctly recognised after a very long wait for exactly that.

He folded his hands and bowed his head - not from any instruction, no one had ever taught him the correct form for this particular occasion - and simply did what seemed, in that moment, to be the only honest thing left to do with his body while his mind was still catching up to the rest of him.

When he lifted his head again, the garden was empty. The guava tree threw its ordinary crooked shadow across the grass, exactly as it always did. Somewhere past the boundary wall, a dog barked twice at nothing and gave it up.

He stood there a while longer anyway, barefoot in the cooling grass, turning a name over in his mind that wasn't Hers - Ananya - the way you turn over the one new fact in a life otherwise already settled, already understood, and find, to your own quiet astonishment, that you have absolutely no idea yet what to do with it except wait, patiently, for it to arrive.
