# Chapter Nineteen: What He's Giving Up

The study light stayed on long after the rest of the apartment had gone dark, long after he'd heard Ananya's door close upstairs and Sandhya's footsteps finally stop pacing the kitchen and settle, unhappily, into their bedroom. Suraj sat at his desk without any particular papers in front of him, a glass of water he hadn't touched sweating a ring onto the wood, and did the only thing he still knew how to do with an evening that had gone entirely out of his hands: he went back through it, slowly, the way he went through a ledger that didn't balance, looking for the one figure that would explain all the others.

Ankush's shouting hadn't told him anything he didn't already know. A young man humiliated in front of strangers shouted; that was simple arithmetic, requiring no particular insight to solve. Niraj's stillness told him rather more, though nothing he hadn't already priced in somewhere over the past two years - the reminder about Wolverine's goodwill delivered so lightly it would have passed, in any other house, for nothing worse than a passing courtesy. Suraj had heard men make that particular kind of remark before, in board meetings, across negotiating tables, always in that same unhurried register, and had learned long ago that the softer the delivery, the higher the number actually being named.

It was Ananya who gave him the figure he'd actually been missing.

*I wasn't thinking about how it looked. I was thinking about him.* Six words, said plainly, without a tremor in them, in a room where everyone else had been shouting or threatening or holding perfectly still out of fear. He had watched his daughter say it and felt something settle in him that no amount of Niraj's careful menace had managed to touch - not because the sentence was dramatic, but because it wasn't. She hadn't sounded like a girl defending a fantasy. She'd sounded like a woman stating a fact she'd stopped needing anyone else to verify years ago.

He turned his glass a slow half-circle on the desk, an old habit, and let himself, for the first time since this whole arrangement began, actually retire the plan he'd been quietly running on for weeks - the hope, dressed up carefully enough that he'd almost convinced himself it was a strategy, that the marriage would simply fail to happen on its own, some slow accumulation of delays and postponements that let him avoid ever having to refuse Niraj outright. That plan had needed one thing to survive: time, moving slowly enough that nobody felt the need to force anything. He understood now, sitting alone with the study lamp buzzing faintly overhead, that he no longer had it. A man like Niraj did not deliver a line like the one he'd delivered this evening unless he'd already decided the delaying was over. Whatever came next was coming regardless of anything Suraj did to slow it.

So he let himself, instead, do the thing he'd been avoiding doing properly for two years: he thought, without flinching from it, about what actually happened when it came.

He knew what he'd read about the man his daughter had chosen - the business daily, the two ventures built from nothing inside five years, the particular quality men in his own circles used when they mentioned Shaktiyuth's name, a respect that had nothing to do with liking him and everything to do with never quite managing to get ahead of him. He knew, too, what he himself had said to Sandhya three days ago, turning it over slowly enough that he'd surprised even himself with how much he meant it: *he isn't someone who gets pushed anywhere he hasn't already decided to go.* Ankush was accustomed to being obeyed. Niraj was accustomed to arranging the board so that he always won before anyone else knew a game was being played. Suraj found, running the numbers on it now with the same flat, unsentimental attention he gave any account that wasn't reconciling, that he did not believe either one of them had the faintest idea what they were actually walking toward. He did not allow himself to picture it further than that. It wasn't his to picture. It was only his to understand that whatever it turned out to be, Niraj and Ankush were unlikely to be the ones left standing at the end of it.

Which left him, unavoidably, at Vikas Limited.

He'd known for a year, since the audit page had surfaced a filing discrepancy he'd wished, more than once since, he'd never gone looking for, exactly what Niraj's stake actually was - not an investment, a leash, bought with money made from a wound Niraj himself had inflicted first. He remembered the exact evening he'd found it: a junior accountant's flagged line item, a Meridian Systems registration address that matched, with a little digging he hadn't strictly needed to do, a Wolverine Industries subsidiary filed three years earlier. He had sat with that discovery in this same chair, at this same hour, and told no one - not the board, not Sandhya, not even, in the end, himself, not fully, not until tonight forced the rest of the accounting due. He had built, in the year since, a careful, private arithmetic around it: hold steady, keep the board calm, wait for an opening to buy the stake back on terms Niraj couldn't easily refuse, and in the meantime give away nothing that would tell his old friend-turned-shareholder that he knew. It was, he could admit to himself now, in the particular honesty a man allows himself only at midnight with no one else in the room, a plan built entirely on Niraj remaining exactly where he currently stood, patient and undiscovered, for as long as it took Suraj to work his way free of him.

That plan required Niraj's position never to change. He no longer believed it wouldn't.

If Niraj fell - and some cold, unsentimental part of Suraj had already stopped pretending he thought otherwise - his stake didn't simply vanish with him. It went to lawyers, to inheritance, to a scramble of claims and counterclaims that could tie the company in litigation for years regardless of who eventually won it, bleeding it exactly as thoroughly as Meridian Systems ever had, just through a different vein. And if Niraj somehow didn't fall, if Suraj's read on the coming days turned out wrong in every particular, then nothing changed at all - the company went on quietly answering to a man who'd manufactured his own way into it, exactly as it had for the year since, and Vikas Limited arrived, a little more slowly, at precisely the same ending.

There was, he made himself admit, a third branch, and he made himself look at it properly rather than skip past it out of some vanity about the kind of man he preferred to think he was: he could still let the marriage proceed. Let Ananya go to Ankush's house as arranged, keep Niraj satisfied, keep the stake friendly rather than hostile, and trade his daughter's whole life for a percentage chance - not even a certainty, only a chance - of saving a company that bore his father's name. He turned that branch over exactly once, the way you turn over a coin you already know is counterfeit, and set it down again. It cost him nothing to refuse. That, more than anything else he worked out that night, was the part he trusted least about himself, and the part he trusted most.

He turned the arithmetic over twice more after that, hunting for some remaining branch where the company survived either way without asking anything further of her, and did not find one. There wasn't a version of the next several weeks, so far as he could work out, in which the business his father had built and he had spent thirty years growing came out the other side of this intact.

He sat with that for a long moment, the water glass sweating its slow ring into the wood, and found, to his own quiet surprise, that once the fact was fully admitted the grief attached to it arrived smaller than he'd have guessed - already halfway spent, somehow, on the year he'd spent not-quite-admitting it to himself.

What remained, once the company was subtracted from the equation entirely, was a much shorter list than he'd have expected two years ago, back when he'd still believed the two things could be kept separate. His daughter. What she'd asked him for, without quite asking: not permission, not blessing, only the door left unlocked when the time came. He had spent thirty years learning to read a balance sheet for the one number that told him what the rest of the columns were actually protecting. He read this one the same way now, and found that once he stopped trying to save both, the answer had never really been in doubt at all.

He would say none of this to her. That much he decided as plainly and as finally as everything else that night - not out of any wish to keep the sacrifice from her, exactly, but because saying it aloud would turn it into a debt she'd feel obliged to spend the rest of her life repaying, and he wanted, whatever else this cost him, to give her a wedding night unweighted by that particular arithmetic. Let her think, if she ever thought of it at all, that her father had simply, quietly, run out of reasons to say no.

He pulled the household's night ledger toward him - the staff roster, the guard's rotation, kept in his own hand out of the same old habit that kept the company's harder numbers in his own hand too - and turned to the week the wedding fell in, reading the names on the gate shift the way he read anything he intended to remember without needing to ask about it twice. It might have been nothing more than the ordinary caution of a man tightening security around his own house in an uncertain week. He made no notes either way. He needed none. He had always been the kind of man who did his most necessary planning without ever once writing it down where another person might come to read it.

Outside, the city had gone as quiet as it ever did. Upstairs, his daughter was finally, he hoped, asleep. Suraj sat a while longer with the ledger open in front of him, the water still untouched, doing arithmetic that would never appear in any account he'd ever have to show another living soul, and did not turn off the light until he was entirely certain of the sum.
